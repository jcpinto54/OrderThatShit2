# 01 — Why people share: the science of virality (applied brief for Order That Shit)

*Compiled 2026-09-29 by a research agent; verify before quoting publicly.*

**TL;DR.** People share to look good, to bond, to help or persuade someone, and to process emotions, and they share more when content leaves them fired up (high arousal). For a funny, positive property the best-supported recipe is high-arousal positive emotion (amusement, surprise, excitement, a little triumph) packed into a personal artifact that makes the sharer look clever. Quiet contentment and sadness spread poorly; outrage spreads mainly in news and politics, costs persuasion, and is partly contested. Network research adds three sobering facts: almost every shared item reaches nobody new, big hits are usually one large broadcast rather than a long chain, and which item hits is hard to predict in advance. So ship many small, personal share units (Shit Finder verdict cards, certificates, 8-second clips) and make the link preview itself the joke. Tie the brand to the recurring "should I buy it?" moment and to Black Friday, seed many communities at once, and reinvest only in what shows early speed. The cathartic goal fits the science: telling others about a good thing amplifies the good feeling (capitalization), as long as the moment feels like a win worth showing off, not a quiet sigh of relief.

**Tags.** **[Robust]**: several independent datasets or methods agree. **[Suggestive]**: one paper or lab, small samples, stated intentions, or correlation only. **[Practitioner lore]**: industry claims, not peer reviewed. **(contested)**: published re-analyses or failed replications exist. Effect sizes are as the authors report them. Sibling briefs: platforms and share UX (04), case studies (05), mystery-order business (06), AI video (07).

## 1. Emotion and arousal

### 1.1 Arousal matters more than good-vs-bad

- **The anchor study.** Berger & Milkman (2012) tracked all 6,956 New York Times homepage articles over three months of 2008; 20% reached the most-emailed list. Per one-standard-deviation (SD) increase, the chance of making the list moved by: anger +34%, awe +30%, anxiety +21%, sadness −16%; positivity +13%; practical value +30%, interest +25%, surprise +14%. For scale, an extra SD of time at the top of the homepage was worth +20%. **[Robust]** as a field pattern.
- **Small lab follow-ups** in the same paper (N = 45–49 each). A funnier version of an ad story (a pork-sausage brand hires a rabbi) raised sharing intent from 2.92 to 3.97 on a 1–7 scale; a sadder news story cut it from 3.80 to 2.39. Felt arousal explained both. **[Suggestive]**.
- **Arousal from anything.** Arousal from emotional clips (N = 93) or even jogging in place (N = 40) made people more willing to share unrelated content (Berger 2011). **[Suggestive]**: small samples, no large direct replication found.
- **Partial replication.** Heimbach & Hinz (2016) re-ran the analysis on a German news magazine across several channels. Virality again rose with positivity and emotionality (especially anger, awe and anxiety) and fell with sadness. Not every effect replicated, and positivity showed an inverted U, so very sweet content underperformed. **[Robust]** direction.
- **Disgust is domain-specific.** It boosted the passing-on of urban legends (Heath, Bell & Sternberg 2001) but had no effect in the NYT data.

### 1.2 Videos and ads: the positive, high-arousal corner wins

- **Nelson-Field, Riebe & Newstead (2013)**, ~800 videos with real daily share counts: arousal was the main driver; valence mattered less. **[Robust]**-ish.
- **Smaller studies agree.** **[Suggestive]**: lab or student samples, intentions.
  - Pleasant-toned ads drew the strongest intent to forward; negative ones the weakest (Eckler & Bolls 2011).
  - Cute and funny clips beat anger and disgust. Anger clips were forwarded more when they seemed to come from a rival university (Guadagno et al. 2013; N = 256 and 163, per BPS Research Digest).
  - Surprise and joy dominated the 25 most-shared ads of 2006–2013 (Dafonte-Gómez 2014).
- **Tellis, MacInnis, Tirunillai & Zhang (2019)**: two field studies (11 emotions, 60+ ad traits) plus a hold-out test. **[Robust]**.
  - Information-heavy ads were shared less, except in risky categories.
  - Amusement, excitement, inspiration and warmth raised sharing; drama (surprise, plot, characters) produced those emotions.
  - Prominent early or persistent branding cut sharing.
  - Sharing peaked at 1.2–1.7 minutes. That is YouTube-era ad data; don't apply it to 8-second clips.
- **Lee, Hosanagar & Nair (2018)**: 106,316 Facebook posts from 782 companies. Humour and emotion went with more engagement. Bare price or deal information went with less, unless paired with personality, though deals did drive clicks. **[Robust]**: large, observational.

### 1.3 Shared is not the same as persuasive

- **Tucker (2015)** combined 400 video ads with 24,000 survey responses from randomised exposure. **[Suggestive]**: one study, strong design.
  - On average, each extra million views came with ~10% lower persuasiveness. Provocative ads buy reach at the cost of persuasion.
  - The exception was ads whose views came from humour rather than outrage.
- **Akpinar & Berger (2017)** used real share data for hundreds of ads, plus experiments. **[Suggestive]**.
  - Emotional ads were shared more; informative ads helped brand evaluations.
  - Emotional ads where the brand is integral to the story got both.
  - Only ~20% of emotional ads made the brand highly integral.

### 1.4 Negativity and outrage: real, but narrower than the hype

- **Brady et al. (2017)**: 563,312 tweets on three hot-button issues; each moral-emotional word went with ~20% more retweets. **(contested)**: Burton, Cruz & Hahn (2021) found this model predicted no better than a deliberately nonsensical one.
- **Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden (2021)**: 2.73M posts from news outlets and members of US Congress. Posts about the political out-group were shared about twice as often, and each out-group word raised share odds by 67%. That beat negative language by ~4.8× and moral-emotional language by ~6.7×. **[Robust]** for politics.
- **Robertson et al. (2023)**: Upworthy's randomised headline tests (~105,000 variants, 370M+ impressions). Each extra negative word raised click-through by 2.3%; positive words lowered it. **[Robust]** and causal, but small per word, and about clicks.
- **Watson et al. (2024)**: 95,282 articles, 579M posts. Links to negative news were 1.91× more likely to be shared. **[Robust]** for news.
- **Vosoughi, Roy & Aral (2018)**: ~126,000 stories on Twitter, 2006–2017. False news spread farther and faster, was more novel, and drew surprise and disgust. **[Robust]** for novelty.
  - Juul & Ugander (2021): the structural differences vanish when cascades of equal size are compared. **(contested)** for structure.
- **Brady et al. (2021)**: likes on outrage posts teach people to post more outrage. Preregistered; 12.7M tweets plus experiments. **[Suggestive]**.
- **Outside news the balance shifts.** Links rated more interesting or positive spread further (Bakshy et al. 2011). Negative tweets spread faster, but positive ones reached larger audiences (Ferrara & Yang 2015). **[Suggestive]**.

### 1.5 What this means for a positive, funny property

- **Target** amusement, surprise, excitement and triumph. Treat calm relief or contentment as the afterglow, not the thing people share.
- **Don't borrow outrage.** It works best for politics and news, it reduces persuasion, and it is toxic for a brand. Your target is a system (marketing, consumer culture), not a group of people.
- **Negative words can still hook** ("My life was bad…"), as long as the payoff flips to a win. Swings between bad and good raise engagement (section 4).

## 2. Why people share: motives

Berger's (2014) review sorts word of mouth into five functions: impression management, emotion regulation, information seeking, social bonding and persuasion. They are mostly self-serving and often unconscious. **[Robust]** as a map.

### Looking good

- **Toubia & Stephen (2013)**, a Twitter field experiment that added followers at random: for most users, how posting made them look mattered more than enjoying posting itself. **[Suggestive]**: one strong study.
- **Barasch & Berger (2014)**, six studies: broadcasting to many made people avoid content that makes them look bad. Narrowcasting to one person shifted focus to the recipient, so they shared more useful things. **[Suggestive]**.
- **Berger & Iyengar (2013)**, ~20,000 conversations plus experiments: written and online channels lead people to mention more interesting things, because they can polish and want to look good. **[Suggestive]**.
- **Berger & Heath (2007)**: in identity-relevant domains (music, hairstyles) people adopt and drop tastes to signal who they are. **[Robust]** concept. Self-enhancement also shapes what people say about their own choices (De Angelis et al. 2012, via Barasch & Berger 2014).

### Talking about yourself is rewarding

- **Tamir & Mitchell (2012).** People spend 30–40% of speech on their own experiences, and self-disclosure activated reward circuitry. Participants gave up ~17% of potential earnings to answer questions about themselves, and ~25% when the answer would be shared. **[Robust]** mechanism, though the stakes were cents.
- **Scholz et al. (2017)**, fMRI: brain signals of self-relevance and social relevance predicted real-world sharing of NYT health articles. **[Suggestive]**.

### Emotion and bonding

- **Rimé (2009)** reviews his group's many studies: most emotional episodes, positive and negative, get talked about, usually soon and repeatedly. A secondary summary puts it at ~88–96% in early studies. **[Robust]**.
- **Sharing is not therapy by itself.** Over nine months, sharing tapered as people recovered, and prolonged sharing marked poorer recovery (Curci & Rimé 2012). **[Suggestive]**.
- **Capitalization.** Telling others about a good event raises positive feelings beyond the event itself, more so when the listener reacts enthusiastically (Gable et al. 2004, four studies). **[Robust]**. For this site, the share is part of the reward.

### Controversy and originality

- **Chen & Berger (2013)**, five studies: talk rises with controversy up to a moderate level, then falls as discomfort outweighs interest. Anonymity, and whether people talk to friends or strangers, shift the curve. **[Suggestive]**.
- **Moldovan, Goldenberg & Chattopadhyay (2011)**: originality drives *how much* people talk, and usefulness decides whether the talk is positive. Original-but-useless products drew more negative word of mouth. **[Suggestive]**.
- **Phelps et al. (2004)** ran three early studies on why people forward emails. The abstract was inaccessible, so its findings are unverified.

### Practitioner survey

NYT Customer Insight Group & Latitude Research (2011), *The Psychology of Sharing*: ethnography, a one-week "sharing deprivation" panel, and a survey of 2,500 medium and heavy sharers. Self-reported motives: 94% weigh usefulness to the recipient, 84% support causes, 78% stay connected, 69% feel involved, 68% define themselves. It also offers six personas (Altruists, Careerists, Hipsters, Boomerangs, Connectors, Selectives). **[Practitioner lore]**: self-report, heavy sharers only, run by a publisher selling ads.

## 3. Frameworks: STEPPS and SUCCESs

| STEPPS principle (Berger 2013, *Contagious*) | Best evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ocial currency: people share what makes them look good or in the know | Toubia & Stephen 2013; Barasch & Berger 2014; Berger & Iyengar 2013 | Well supported |
| **T**riggers: frequent cues keep a brand top of mind | Berger & Schwartz 2011; Berger & Fitzsimons 2008 | Supported, especially for *ongoing* talk; modest effects |
| **E**motion: high arousal | Berger & Milkman 2012; Tellis et al. 2019 | Well supported |
| **P**ublic: visible behaviour gets copied and discussed | Berger & Schwartz 2011; Bond et al. 2012 | Reasonably supported |
| **P**ractical value | Berger & Milkman 2012 (+30%); Barasch & Berger 2014 | Supported |
| **S**tories: the brand rides inside a narrative ("Trojan horse") | Closest test is Akpinar & Berger 2017 (brand-integral ads) | Weakest direct evidence |

### Critique of STEPPS

A useful checklist, built mostly from one lab's studies plus case anecdotes (with survivorship bias). No published test shows that scoring content on STEPPS predicts what goes viral. It lists ingredients, not a recipe, and ignores network position, seeding and luck (section 5), which often dominate.

### SUCCESs

Heath & Heath (2007), *Made to Stick*: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. It is about memorability more than sharing. Its parts rest on real findings: curiosity gaps (Loewenstein 1994), easy concrete language holding attention (Berger, Moe & Schweidel 2023), and emotional selection of urban legends (Heath et al. 2001). The package as a whole was never tested. Use it as an editing checklist for scripts and verdict copy. **[Practitioner lore]** as a model; parts **[Robust]**.

## 4. Hooks, curiosity and attention

### Curiosity

**Information gap** (Loewenstein 1994): curiosity arises when attention lands on a specific gap between what you know and what you want to know, and people act to close it. Foundational theory.

### Clickbait is a small, unreliable edge

- **What exists.** Blom & Hansen (2015) described 100,000 headlines from ten Danish news sites: "forward-reference" lures ("This is why…") grew with commercial and tabloid pressure. They measured use, not effects.
- **Mixed effects.** Most clickbait features raised clicks on the news app Blendle, but couldn't predict winners (Kuiken et al. 2017). Plain summary headlines beat some curiosity headlines in a lab and on nine local news sites (Scacco & Muddiman 2020). Clickbait may lower perceived credibility (Molyneux & Coddington 2020), and it had no effect on trust or learning (Munger et al. 2020).
- **Gligorić et al. (2023)**, a preregistered study of 24,333 Upworthy headline pairs. Negative-emotion words, "I"/"he"/"she", indefinite articles and slightly longer headlines helped; "we" hurt. Positive words, readability and "you" did nothing. The best model picked the winner only 54% of the time (58% at best), against 50% chance.

**[Robust]** conclusion: wording tweaks are small beer; the idea and the image matter more. The Upworthy archive has 32,487 experiments (Matias et al. 2021); see also Banerjee & Urminsky (2025).

### Surprise grabs attention; rising joy holds it

- Statistically surprising events attract the eye (Itti & Baldi 2009). **[Robust]**.
- Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters (2012) combined facial coding, eye tracking and skip data. Surprise concentrated attention, and *rising* joy kept viewers from skipping: speed of the rise mattered more than its level. **[Suggestive]**.
- In Teixeira, Picard & el Kaliouby (2014; 82 ads, 178 viewers), entertainment had an inverted-U relation to purchase intent. Only entertainment *after* the brand appeared helped. **[Suggestive]**.
- Berger, Moe & Schweidel (2023; 600,000 reading sessions, 35,000+ pieces): easy, concrete language and arousing or uncertain emotions (anxious, exciting, hopeful) kept people reading; sad language lost them. **[Robust]**-ish.
- Berger, Kim & Meyer (2021): emotional swings between good and bad raised engagement across 4,000+ movies and 30,000+ articles, confirmed by experiments. **[Suggestive]**.

### Attention is the scarce input

- In Weng et al.'s (2012) model, limited attention plus network structure reproduced Twitter's extreme popularity inequality with *no* quality differences between memes. **[Suggestive]**: a model. So crowded windows like Black Friday week raise the share of luck.
- A later claim that low-quality information is as viral as high-quality (Qiu et al. 2017) was **retracted** in 2019.
- Platform guidance stresses a video's first ~2–3 seconds (**[Practitioner lore]**; see 04). The "8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish" statistic traces to no identifiable research (BBC *More or Less*, 2017).

## 5. Network structure and luck

- **Most shares go nowhere.** Goel, Watts & Goldstein (2012), seven domains including Twitter news and videos: 73–96% of shared items spread to nobody, 94–99% of adoptions happened one step from the original sharer, and under 1% of cascades passed seven people. **[Robust]**.
- **Big usually means broadcast.** Goel, Anderson, Hofman & Watts (2016), ~1 billion Twitter diffusion events: long person-to-person chains were rare at every size, and popularity came mostly from the single largest broadcast (though every mix occurred). **[Robust]**.
- **Luck compounds.** **[Robust]**.
  - In MusicLab (Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006; 14,341 people), showing download counts made success more unequal and less predictable. The best songs rarely flopped and the worst rarely won; anything else happened.
  - Faked popularity became self-fulfilling for most songs, but the best recovered, and total downloads fell (Salganik & Watts 2008; 12,207 people).
- **Hard ceiling on prediction.** Even rich models explained under half the variance in Twitter cascade size (Martin et al. 2016). **[Robust]**.
- **But prediction works once something starts.** **[Robust]**.
  - After a Facebook photo's first five reshares, whether it would double was predicted with ~80% accuracy (AUC 0.88). Speed was the best signal, and early breadth beat depth (Cheng et al. 2014).
  - Memes that reach many separate communities early are the ones that go viral (Weng, Menczer & Ahn 2013).
- **Influencers (contested, but reconcilable).**
  - Simulations say big cascades usually need a critical mass of easily influenced people, not a few "influentials" (Watts & Dodds 2007).
  - Bakshy et al. (2011; 1.6M Twitter users, 74M diffusion events): big cascades came from high-follower accounts with past hits, but *which* one would cascade was unpredictable. Many cheap "ordinary influencers" were most cost-effective under most assumptions.
  - Hinz et al. (2011; field experiments plus a real campaign, >200,000 customers): seeding well-connected people worked up to 8× better, because hubs take part more and reach more, not because they persuade better.
  - Net: hubs buy reach; price decides.
- **Viral product features.** Aral & Walker (2011), a randomised trial of a Facebook app (9,687 users, 1.4M friends). Automatic passive broadcast notifications raised adoption among friends by 246%; active personalised invites added 98% more. Invites worked better per message, but broadcasts were used far more. **[Suggestive]**: one large trial.
- **Big-seed math.** Reach N people directly, and let each bring in R more, with R below 1. Total reach is then about N ÷ (1 − R) (Watts & Peretti 2007). R = 0.5 doubles reach; R = 0.2 adds 25%. Sharing pays even without going "viral" (R above 1). **[Robust]**: arithmetic.

## 6. Social proof, public visibility and triggers

- **Triggers.** **[Suggestive]** to **[Robust]**.
  - Berger & Schwartz (2011) used conversations about 300+ products, a multi-city field experiment and a lab study. Interesting products got only *immediate* buzz. Products cued by everyday surroundings, or used in public, got more talk both right away and months later. Linking the restaurant chain Boston Market to "dinner" raised talk among people who hadn't made that link.
  - Frequent exposure to related cues makes products more accessible, liked and chosen (Berger & Fitzsimons 2008, six studies). Orange-branded products were more top of mind the day before Halloween than a week after.
- **Black Friday** is a giant annual trigger for your exact theme: everyone is already thinking about ordering, and journalists need an angle. It is also the year's most crowded attention window (Weng et al. 2012). *(Inference.)*
- **Making private behaviour public.** In a trial on 61M Facebook users, showing people which friends had voted changed real voting. The social ripple beat the direct effect and ran mostly through close friends (Bond et al. 2012). **[Robust]**. Ordering is private; a certificate makes it public.
- **Social proof and herding.** On a news-sharing site (~100,000 comments), one random early up-vote raised later positive ratings by 32% and final scores by 25%; fake down-votes got corrected (Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013). MusicLab shows the same. **[Robust]**-ish.
  - Real counts ("N certificates issued") are legitimate social proof. Fake tickers only work as obvious satire.
  - Once money is involved, fake popularity can be a misleading practice under EU consumer law (see 05, 06).

## 7. Recent work (2019–2026): memes, short video, TikTok-era sharing

- **The old findings hold in new data.** That covers emotion (Tellis et al. 2019), negativity in news (Robertson et al. 2023; Watson et al. 2024) and attention and emotional swings (Berger et al. 2021, 2023). Negativity's edge is clearest in news and politics.
- **Short video and memes.**
  - Ling et al. (2022; 400 TikTok videos): creator follower count was the strongest predictor. Close-up and medium shots, on-screen text and point of view also mattered.
  - Ling et al. (2021; 100 image memes from one fringe forum): close-ups, a clear character and visible emotion helped; long text and no clear subject hurt.
  - **[Suggestive]**: small samples.
- **Imitation is the new share.** Zulli & Zulli (2022) argue that TikTok's design (reusable sounds, duets, templates) makes imitation the main mode of taking part. Shifman (2014) defines memes as families of remixed variants. **[Suggestive]**: conceptual.
- **AI labels cost a little trust.** In two preregistered experiments (N = 4,976), labelling headlines "AI-generated" lowered their perceived accuracy and people's willingness to share them, even when true or human-made (Altay & Gilardi 2024). The effect was a third the size of a "false" label, and came from assuming no human was involved. **[Suggestive]**: headlines, not comedy.
- **Methods warning.** Burton et al. (2021) and Juul & Ugander (2021) show how huge observational datasets mislead. Your own A/B tests beat borrowed coefficients.

## 8. What this means for Order That Shit

Confidence: **High** = directly supported by robust findings. **Medium** = supported, but extrapolated to your format. **Bet** = my inference; test it.

### 1. Engineer the spike, then the afterglow

"Now that I ordered some shit, I feel good" is contentment, a low-arousal feeling. In experiments, low arousal produced less sharing than high arousal (Berger 2011; Berger & Milkman 2012). Deliver it inside a high-arousal moment: confetti, a fanfare, an absurdly grand certificate ("Certified Decisive Person, Black Friday 2026"). Then put the sincere line last: *"You're allowed to want things."* Warmth and inspiration do raise sharing (Tellis et al. 2019), and a joke-then-sincere swing adds engagement (Berger, Kim & Meyer 2021).

**Confidence:** High (direction), Bet (wording).

### 2. The share unit is *my* verdict and *my* certificate, not the homepage

Give every Shit Finder verdict and certificate its own URL, with a server-rendered preview: "Problem: [my words] → Analysis: 100% → Verdict: ORDER THAT SHIT", plus the keyword-specific line. Talking about yourself is rewarding (Tamir & Mitchell 2012), identity signals get shared (Berger & Heath 2007; Toubia & Stephen 2013), and telling people about a win amplifies it (Gable et al. 2004).

**Confidence:** High (mechanism), Medium (format).

### 3. The preview is the content

59% of news links shared on Twitter were never clicked by anyone (Gabielkov et al. 2016), so most people will only ever see your card. Make it legible as a thumbnail: one big face or seal, few words, punchline visible. Close-ups and a clear subject help (Ling et al. 2021).

**Confidence:** High.

### 4. Broadcast-safe by default

When posting to many, people avoid anything that makes them look bad (Barasch & Berger 2014). The joke must make the sharer look witty, never broke, lonely or compulsive. Keep typed problems private unless the user opts in, offer flattering preset problems and titles, and aim the satire at the marketing machine, not the buyer.

**Confidence:** Medium-high.

### 5. Add one-tap narrowcasting

One-to-one sharing makes people pick what's useful to the recipient (Barasch & Berger 2014). Add prefilled WhatsApp, iMessage or DM buttons: "Send to the friend who's had the same thing in their cart since March", or "Write a permission slip for [name]". This also matches how platforms weight sends and DMs (04).

**Confidence:** Medium.

### 6. Seed each joke into its tribe, all at once, and chase one big broadcast

People share in-jokes that show off expertise or identity (Berger & Heath 2007; Berger & Iyengar 2013), and early spread across many communities predicts virality (Weng et al. 2013). Map features to tribes and post each in the same window:

- The Science™ (r = 1.00) → data people.
- The dark-pattern parodies (endless sale, rising stock counter, idle nag, exit modal, cookie banner, fake ticker) → UX, dev and marketing people.
- Marcus while prod burns → engineers.
- Linda at 2am → insomniacs and parents.
- Gary → relationship humour.
- The CEO → startup satire.

Many ordinary seeds are the cost-effective base (Bakshy et al. 2011). But big hits usually come from one large broadcast (Goel et al. 2016), and well-connected people take part more (Hinz et al. 2011). So pitch the "anti-Black-Friday satire" angle to newsletters, journalists and mid-size creators well before the day.

**Confidence:** Medium-high.

### 7. Own the "should I buy it?" trigger

Cued products get *ongoing* talk (Berger & Schwartz 2011; Berger & Fitzsimons 2008). Turn each punchline into a reaction asset (GIF, sticker, 3-second clip: Gary's line, an "ORDER THAT SHIT" stamp). Then it's one tap away whenever someone in a group chat hesitates over a purchase. The domain itself is a reply.

**Confidence:** Medium (cue science), Bet (whether people adopt the assets).

### 8. Black Friday: arrive early, drop daily

Black Friday (27 Nov) and Cyber Monday (30 Nov) are your theme's annual trigger, but also peak competition for attention (Weng et al. 2012). Go live with hero assets ~2–3 weeks early, so roundups can include you and early social proof builds. Then drop a new verdict pack or clip daily through Cyber Monday ("doorbuster verdicts"), giving people a fresh reason to reshare.

**Confidence:** Bet.

### 9. Build a portfolio, then follow the speed

Which item hits is hard to predict (Salganik et al. 2006; Martin et al. 2016), and most items reach nobody (Goel et al. 2012). Make 30–60 cheap variations (persona × problem × punchline) and release them in waves. In the first hours, track shares per 1,000 views and their speed. Then recut, sequel or boost only the top ~10%, since early speed and breadth predict growth (Cheng et al. 2014; Weng et al. 2013). At ~$1–2 per clip (07), 50 variations cost roughly $50–100.

**Confidence:** High.

### 10. Raise R with frictionless and passive sharing

Reach ≈ N ÷ (1 − R), so removing friction pays even without going "viral" (Watts & Peretti 2007). Use the native share sheet, pre-rendered images and copy-link. Add passive signals that travel with each share (Aral & Walker 2011): a certificate number ("Certificate #48,213"), an opt-in "Wall of the Certified", and real counters next to the obviously fake ticker. Measure R per share unit: arrivals from shared links ÷ viewers.

**Confidence:** High (math), Medium (feature effects).

### 11. Clip anatomy: pattern break → escalation → brand-integral punchline

- Open on the surprising image or line, not the setup (Itti & Baldi 2009; Teixeira et al. 2012).
- Swing bad → absurd → twist (Berger, Kim & Meyer 2021) and end on rising joy.
- Make "order that shit" the punchline, not an opening logo. Early branding cuts sharing (Tellis et al. 2019), while brand-integral emotion gets shares *and* recall (Akpinar & Berger 2017).
- Keep sad beats short; sad language loses attention (Berger, Moe & Schweidel 2023).

Gary's "The shit had tracking. She did not." is the template.

**Confidence:** Medium.

### 12. Moderate controversy, no enemies

Invite light debate that doesn't cause discomfort ("Is ordering at 2am self-care?"); moderate controversy maximises talk (Chen & Berger 2013). Skip political or out-group targets: they drive engagement (Rathje et al. 2021) but kill persuasion (Tucker 2015) and the brand.

**Confidence:** Medium.

### 13. Give the absurdity a useful core

Original-but-useless draws negative word of mouth (Moldovan et al. 2011), and practical value adds shares (Berger & Milkman 2012). Hide one genuinely helpful thing inside the satire: a sendable permission slip, or a rule of thumb ("Wanted it for three months and it's under €30? You're fine."). This serves the permission-giving goal honestly.

**Confidence:** Bet.

### 14. User-made testimonials: fixed template, labelled, cheapest first

- Creators overvalue what they make and expect others to agree (IKEA effect; Norton et al. 2012). Self-disclosure is rewarding, and TikTok culture spreads by imitating templates (Zulli & Zulli 2022).
- Start with a still portrait plus a quote card in a fixed format ("My problem: ___. I ordered that shit."). Offer paid or limited 8-second clips later.
- Label AI output so it reads as human-written comedy. An unexplained "AI" label mildly cuts sharing (Altay & Gilardi 2024), and disclosure rules apply (07).

**Confidence:** Bet.

*Mystery order:* a real surprise creates a curiosity gap and a public unboxing moment, but see 06 on why the €1 version is not advised.

## 9. Common myths and things that don't hold up

- **"Outrage and negativity always win."** Mostly true for news and politics, and even there per-word effects are small and partly contested (Burton et al. 2021). For entertainment and brands, positive high-arousal content does well, and outrage-driven reach costs persuasion (Tucker 2015).
- **"Any emotion drives shares."** Low-arousal feelings spread worse. Sadness reduced sharing, and contentment clips led to less sharing than amusement or anxiety clips (Berger & Milkman 2012; Berger 2011).
- **"Viral means an exponential chain."** Almost all spread happens one step from the sharer; big hits are mostly one big broadcast (Goel et al. 2012, 2016).
- **"Find the right influencer."** Which account will spark a cascade is unpredictable (Bakshy et al. 2011). Simulations favour a critical mass of ordinary people (Watts & Dodds 2007), and hubs help through reach, not magic (Hinz et al. 2011).
- **"STEPPS or SUCCESs is a formula."** They are checklists. No test shows they predict virality, and unpredictability plus network position weigh heavily (Salganik et al. 2006; Martin et al. 2016).
- **"Curiosity-gap headlines reliably win."** Effects are small and inconsistent, and clickbait can hurt credibility. Wording barely predicts winners: 54% vs 50% chance, and putting "you" in headlines had no effect (Gligorić et al. 2023).
- **"Attention spans are 8 seconds, less than a goldfish's."** No traceable source (BBC 2017).
- **"59% of people share without reading."** Actually, 59% of news links shared on Twitter got zero clicks (Gabielkov et al. 2016). It's about links, not people, but the lesson holds: the preview is the content.
- **"Low-quality information is as viral as high-quality."** The paper claiming this was retracted (Qiu et al. 2017; retracted 2019).
- **"Going viral sells."** Reach and persuasion can trade off (Tucker 2015). Emotional ads help the brand only when the brand is built into the story (Akpinar & Berger 2017).
- **"Fake popularity is harmless growth hacking."** It can become self-fulfilling but lowered total engagement (Salganik & Watts 2008). Fake negative signals get corrected (Muchnik et al. 2013). In real commerce, it's a legal risk in the EU.
