# Case studies: absurd Black Friday stunts, joke products, mystery commerce and viral AI

*Compiled 2026-09-29 by a research agent; verify before quoting publicly.*

Labels: *(self-reported)* = the company's own figure; *(unverified)* = not confirmed by a reputable source; *(via Wikipedia)* = only a Wikipedia summary of press reports was checked (the web-search quota ran out mid-research). Bracketed numbers point to the sources at the end.

## TL;DR

- **Cards Against Humanity (CAH) is the master precedent.** Every Black Friday since 2013 (13 so far; 2020 was a straight donation) it has run one absurd idea, a real transaction and a number the press can print. 2014's "Bullshit" ($6 boxes of real bull feces) sold 30,000 in ~30 minutes but left only ~$6,000 after shipping, all donated [3]. 2015's "pay $5, get nothing" raised $71,145 from 11,248 people [1][5]. It's marketing spend, not revenue.
- **Joke products sell when buying one is the joke the buyer retells.** Pet Rock (~1.5M units, 1975) [23][24], the Million Dollar Homepage ($1,037,100, 2005) [25], Not-a-Flamethrower (20,000 × $500, 2018) [28].
- **Anti–Black Friday works when it visibly costs the brand.** Patagonia donated 100% of its 2016 Black Friday sales and did $10M against $2M expected [15][16]. REI closed its stores in 2015 and reported +26% online traffic and 1M+ new members [17] *(self-reported)*.
- **Mystery commerce is huge but fragile.** Pop Mart's 2025 revenue was RMB 37.1bn, 38% of it from the Labubu line [40]. Yet cheap mystery boxes look like a known scam format [47][48], and EU authorities pursued Temu for fake discounts, false scarcity and forced "spin the wheel" games [54].
- **AI content is loved when openly absurd and hated when it fakes prestige or reality.** Italian brainrot and Kalshi's ~$2,000 Veo 3 NBA Finals ad spread [64][66]. Coca-Cola's AI Christmas ads drew backlash, and McDonald's Netherlands pulled its own [69]. "Slop" was Merriam-Webster's 2025 word of the year [69]. 82% of ad execs think young consumers like AI ads; 45% do [71].
- **For Order That Shit (OTS): on a site where everything is fake, the one real thing is the story.** The flagship is "Order ONE Thing Friday": numbered authorizations, an honest live counter and a recap. Add one real-money element (a €1-for-nothing charity doorbuster or a capped physical "That Shit™" drop), plus AI-video participation if budget allows (§9).

## 1. Cards Against Humanity's Black Friday, 2013–2025

The formula is the same every year: one absurd idea, its own microsite, a real transaction, a printable number, and often a donation. Co-founder Max Temkin framed it as disgust with the Black Friday spectacle [3]. Year list cross-checked against [13].

**Why it works**
- The joke is built into the transaction, so buyers become co-conspirators who retell it.
- Everything is real: the poop ships, the hole exists, the Birkin is genuine. That's what makes the press repeat it.
- The numbers are small but printable.
- It loses money on purpose. The 2020 budget was $250k, and Bullshit kept ~3% of its revenue. After 13 years, fans wait for it.
- Later years added CAPTCHAs and lotteries against bots, a ready template for limited drops.

**Lesson:** Black Friday is a comedy stage. Bring one bit, a real transaction and a number, and budget it as marketing.

*Relevance:* "Bullshit" is the nearest ancestor of a brand called Order That Shit, which is exactly why re-running it would read as a copy. Shipping manure would also bring EU animal by-product rules into play (ask the legal brief). Borrow the mechanics, not the poop.

## 2. Anti–Black Friday brand plays

**Patagonia, "Don't Buy This Jacket" (2011).**
- **What:** A full-page NYT ad on Black Friday listing the jacket's environmental cost.
- **Numbers:** It cost $57,000. Then-CEO Casey Sheahan estimated $40–50M of free publicity, and sales hit new highs in later years [14]. The often-repeated "+30%" has no primary source I could find *(unverified)*, and causation is unproven.
- **Why:** A costly signal that matched how the brand already behaved (repair, resale), on the loudest shopping day of the year.
- **Lesson:** A contrarian message earns press only if the brand's behaviour backs it.

**Patagonia, 100% of Black Friday sales to the planet (2016).**
- **What:** All global Black Friday sales pledged to grassroots environmental groups.
- **Numbers:** It expected ~$2M and did $10M, with "thousands" of first-time customers [15][16] *(self-reported)*.
- **Lesson:** A charity kicker gives people permission to buy, and can multiply sales and new customers.

**REI #OptOutside (2015–).**
- **What:** All ~143 stores closed on Black Friday; ~12,000 staff paid to go outside.
- **Numbers:** REI reported 1.4M social media hits, 175+ partner organizations, online traffic +26% over the weekend, FY2015 sales +9.3% and 1M+ new co-op members [17] *(self-reported; correlation)*.
- **Since:** It won the Titanium Grand Prix at Cannes 2016 [18] and became permanent in 2022. In 2025 (year 10), 195 stores closed and 14,000 employees were paid, with 150,000+ paid days off since 2015 [19].
- **Lesson:** An absence can be the stunt, if it's credible and repeated.

**Buy Nothing Day (Adbusters, 1992–).**
- **What:** Started in Vancouver and moved to the day after US Thanksgiving in 1997. Major US networks refused its ads. By 2001 it was observed in 35+ countries [20].
- **Why it stayed small:** It remained a protest with nothing to share or buy into.
- **Lesson:** Abstinence alone doesn't travel; a transaction or an artifact does (my inference).

**Newer plays (2020–2025).**
- **What:** Allbirds raised prices on Black Friday 2020 ("break tradition, not the planet"). Deciem closed its stores and site and ran 23% off all November instead. Freitag closed its online store [21]. IKEA's "Buy Back Friday" (2020) bought used furniture back in 27 countries, for store credit up to 50% of the original price [22].
- **Lesson:** Sincere anti–Black Friday is now a crowded genre; the twist is the news.

*Relevance:* OTS can't play Patagonia, because it sells nothing, and it shouldn't imitate CAH. Its honest core is permission to finally order the one thing you actually want. That sits between "buy less" and "mock the ritual", and nobody owns it.

## 3. Joke products that actually sold

**Pet Rock (1975).**
- **What:** Gary Dahl boxed beach rocks with straw and a care manual.
- **Numbers:** ~1.5M sold at ~$4 in about six months, ~95¢ profit each. Dahl became a millionaire, and A24 licensed a 2023 revival [23][24].
- **Why:** The packaging was the joke, which made it a perfect gag gift.
- **Lesson:** The object can be almost nothing if the packaging commits to the story.

**The Million Dollar Homepage (2005).**
- **What:** Alex Tew sold 1M pixels at $1 each, in 10×10 blocks.
- **Numbers:** 999,000 pixels went in ~5 months, and the last 1,000 were auctioned for $38,100, for a gross of $1,037,100. It took off after a press release sent at ~$1k in sales [25][26]. Copycats followed within weeks. By 2019, ~40% of its links were dead [25].
- **Lesson:** Sell participation in the artifact; the public progress number is the story.

**I Am Rich (2008).**
- **What:** A $999.99 iPhone app that showed a red gem.
- **Numbers:** 8 copies sold before Apple pulled it within a day [27].
- **Lesson:** Pure conspicuous absurdity sells a handful and a lot of headlines.

**Not-a-Flamethrower (The Boring Company, 2018).**
- **What:** A merch "flamethrower", renamed to avoid customs trouble.
- **Numbers:** 20,000 at $500 sold within days (≈$10M implied) [28].
- **Lesson:** A limited, absurd drop from a known persona turns attention into cash fast.

**MSCHF (2019–).**
- **What:** An art collective that sells numbered "drops".
- **Numbers:** Satan Shoes (2021): 666 pairs at $1,018, sold out in minutes, then a Nike lawsuit settled with returns. Big Red Boots at $350 (2023). A microscopic Louis Vuitton-style bag auctioned for $63k+ (2023). It has run an ad agency, Applied MSCHF, since August 2025 [29].
- **Lesson:** Numbered drops plus provocation get a sell-out and press. Legal risk is part of the price.

**Liquid Death (2019–).**
- **What:** Canned water marketed like a metal band. The founder starts from the dumbest possible idea and treats the company as entertainment that happens to sell drinks [32]. Fans "sell their soul" in a mock contract to join its club. 100 skateboards containing Tony Hawk's blood sold at $500 almost instantly [31].
- **Numbers:** $263M in retail sales in 2023, and a $1.4B valuation after a $67M raise in March 2024 [30] *(self-reported)*.
- **Lesson:** Commit to the bit at every touchpoint (contracts, merch, FAQ). The marketing is the product.

**Dollar Shave Club (2012).**
- **What:** A $4,500 video starring the founder.
- **Numbers:** It crashed the site and brought 12,000 orders in 48 hours [33][34]. Unilever paid $1B in cash in 2016 [34].
- **Lesson:** One very funny, very cheap explainer beats polish.

**Toilet humour that sold (Harmon Brothers).**
- **Poo-Pourri, "Girls Don't Poop" (2013):** The company was doing ~$8M a year. Inventory sold out in four days with $4M in backorders, and sales passed $27M within a year [35] *(founder, self-reported; the often-cited ~$25k shoot and view counts are unverified)*.
- **Squatty Potty's unicorn (2015):** ~$500k all-in, 30M views in a week, and revenue 600% above projection [36]. By January 2016, online sales were up 600% and retail up 200% [37].
- **Lesson:** Absurd and taboo, but still explaining the product: it spreads *and* sells. Have stock ready.

**Old Spice, "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" (2010, Wieden+Kennedy).**
- **What:** It won the Cannes Film Grand Prix and an Emmy [39]. In July came the "Responses": personalized videos answering fans and celebrities over ~2.5 days (widely cited count: 186), each shot in under 15 minutes.
- **Numbers:** Adweek's tally: 1.5B impressions, 130M views, and body wash sales +55% over three months. W+K's own case study claimed +107% in one month [38].
- **Lesson:** Real-time personalized replies make the audience the cast, and AI now makes that cheap.

## 4. Mystery boxes and surprise commerce

**Pop Mart / Labubu (2024–2026).**
- **What:** Blind boxes hide which figure you get, with rare "secret" variants. Lisa (Blackpink) posting her Labubu in April 2024 lit the fuse, and bag charms, unboxings, queues and fights followed. A 1.2 m Labubu fetched ~$170k at a Beijing auction in June 2025 [44].
- **Numbers:** 2025 revenue RMB 37.12bn (+185%), net profit RMB 13.01bn, and The Monsters (Labubu) RMB 14.16bn (+366%, 38% of revenue) [40].
- **The downside:** Resale premiums cooled from September 2025. One mini-Labubu set fell 24% from its peak, and Pop Mart welcomed that after adding supply [42][43]. Shares fell >22% on results day over dependence on one IP [41], and "Lafufu" fakes spread [44].
- **Why it worked:** Surprise, collectability, public display, scarcity.
- **Lesson:** The unboxing is the marketing. Hype is short, and supply decides the ending.

**Fukubukuro (Japan).**
- **What:** New Year "lucky bags" sold below the value of their contents, an Edo-era custom [46]. Apple Japan sold them for years, some with MacBook Airs inside [45].
- **Lesson:** A mystery tied to a ritual and a guaranteed-value floor feels like a treat, not a gamble.

**The dark side of cheap mystery boxes.**
- **What:** "Unclaimed package", "lost luggage" and "return pallet" ads at a few dollars are a documented scam format [47]. BBB complaints against one "unclaimed mail" seller describe $219 boxes of dollar-store trinkets [48]. MrBeast reportedly spent ~$500k on mystery boxes for a 2021 video [49] *(views unverified)*.
- **Lesson:** A €1 mystery box looks like a scam until proven otherwise, so over-communicate legitimacy.

**Wish.**
- **What:** Its "expectation vs reality" memes made it shorthand for disappointing knockoffs.
- **Numbers:** Valued at $11.2B in 2019 and sold to Qoo10 for $173M in 2024. France delisted it from search and app stores in 2021 [50][51].
- **Lesson:** When disappointment is the joke *on* the buyer, the meme becomes the brand.

**Temu and haul culture.**
- **What:** "Shop like a billionaire" Super Bowl ads in 2023, then five spots plus $15M in giveaways and coupons in 2024 [52]. Hauls and #TikTokMadeMeBuyIt (billions of views) made buying on camera a genre [53].
- **The EU response:** The CPC network (November 2024) flagged fake discounts, false scarcity and deadlines, forced "spin the wheel" gamification and suspect reviews [54]. The Commission found DSA breaches on illegal products in July 2025, followed by a €200M fine in May 2026 *(via Wikipedia)* [55].
- **Lesson:** The dark patterns OTS parodies are live EU enforcement targets. Never bolt them onto a real sale.

**Rules to know (details in the legal brief).**
- **China:** SAMR's blind-box guideline (June 2023) bans sales to under-8s, requires guardian consent above that, and requires disclosure of values, rules and odds [56].
- **UK:** The ASA ruled in March 2024 that ads hiding a game's loot boxes are misleading, because chance-based purchases are material information [57].

## 5. Brand personality (transferable bits only)

- **Duolingo "Duo died" (mid-February 2025).** Duolingo killed its mascot with a Cybertruck, then let users revive him with XP; they logged 50.9bn XP [59]. Similarweb saw Android downloads +38% the next day [58]. Two months later, its "AI-first" memo flooded its TikTok (6.7M followers) and Instagram (4.1M) with anger, and it temporarily wiped its posts [60].
  **Lesson:** Tie the stunt to your core action (for OTS, an authorization). Don't burn the persona with off-brand behaviour, above all "AI replacing people".
- **Wendy's.** Its sarcastic roasts became a textbook case [61]. In #NuggsForCarter (2017), it set an impossible 18M-retweet goal for a year of nuggets. The teen fell short with millions of retweets, and Wendy's gave him the nuggets anyway [62].
  **Lesson:** Set an absurd public goal, then be generous.
- **Ryanair on TikTok.** It uses the "plane with a face" format to mock its own reputation. Dazed notes it only jokes about safe topics [63].
  **Lesson:** Punch at yourself or at "marketing", never at customers.

## 6. AI-generated content that went viral (2024–2026)

**Kalshi's NBA Finals ad (June 2025).**
- **What:** A deliberately chaotic Veo 3 + ChatGPT montage that aired during the Finals; reactions were mixed [64]. Widely credited to AI filmmaker PJ Accetturo and said to have been made in about two days *(not re-verified)*.
- **Numbers:** About $2,000 [64].
- **Why it worked:** It never pretended to be real or prestigious. The cost was the story.

**Veo 3 formats (mid-2025).**
- **What:** Fake street interviews and fake haul videos became the default AI formats, and the model tended to repeat the same joke [65].
- **Lesson:** Formats saturate in weeks.

**Italian brainrot (January 2025–).**
- **What:** AI hybrid creatures (a shark in Nikes, a crocodile-bomber) with pseudo-Italian AI voiceover. By spring it was global, especially with Gen Alpha. It spawned the Roblox hit *Steal a Brainrot*, a Panini sticker album, card games, Fortnite skins (April 2026) and brand imitations, plus controversies and a copyright fight over one character [66].
- **Numbers:** An early video drew ~7M views [66].
- **Why it worked:** Nonsense, named characters and endless remixing. Kids co-author the lore.

**Will Smith eating spaghetti (2023–).**
- **What:** A grotesque early AI clip became the informal benchmark for AI video. Smith parodied it himself in February 2024 [67].
- **Lesson:** Embrace the jank and let the subject in on it.

**Sora 2 (September 2025 to 2026).**
- **What:** A TikTok-style app built on "cameos" of you and your friends. It ran into copyright and deepfake fights, and unions attacked a $1B Disney deal.
- **Numbers:** Users reportedly peaked around 1M, then fell below 500k, at ~$1M a day to run. OpenAI announced the shutdown in March 2026 and closed the app on April 26, 2026 *(via Wikipedia)* [68].
- **Lesson:** Don't build a campaign on one tool's hype cycle.

**The backlash.**
- Coca-Cola's AI remakes of its holiday ads (2024, and again in 2025) drew criticism from viewers and artists.
- McDonald's Netherlands' AI Christmas ad (December 2025) was pulled.
- Svedka's AI Super Bowl LX ad (February 2026) was widely mocked.
- A24's AI *Civil War* posters (2024) showed scenes that aren't in the film.
- Fake "welfare fraud" clips made with Sora (2025) fooled viewers.
- Merriam-Webster and the American Dialect Society both named "slop" their 2025 word of the year [69].

**Audience data.**
- **Pew** (June 2025, 5,023 US adults) [70]:
  - 50% are more concerned than excited about AI.
  - 76% say it's very important to know whether content was made by AI or a human.
  - 53% aren't confident they can tell.
- **IAB/Sonata** (October 2025–January 2026; 505 Gen Z and Millennial consumers, 104 executives) [71]:
  - 82% of execs think these consumers like AI ads; 45% actually do.
  - 39% of Gen Z feel negative about AI ads, against 20% of Millennials.
  - 30% of Gen Z call brands that use AI "inauthentic".
  - Yet 73% say knowing an ad used AI would raise their purchase intent or leave it unchanged.

| Loved / effective | Derided / pulled |
|---|---|
| The absurdity is the point (brainrot, Kalshi) | Pastiche of a cherished human tradition (Coca-Cola, McDonald's NL Christmas) |
| Nobody could mistake it for real | Deceives or misrepresents (Sora fakes, A24 posters) |
| Cheap, fast, many; the low cost is part of the joke | Expensive-looking "prestige" that reads as cost-cutting |
| Participatory: remixes, cameos, adoptable characters | Passive broadcast |
| The subject or creator is in on it (Will Smith) | The brand says "AI-first" while fans value people (Duolingo) |

**Lesson:** AI works as the medium of a joke everyone is in on. It fails as a cheaper substitute for sincerity or truth.

## 7. One-joke websites

- **One Million Checkboxes (June 2024).** Built in two days on a $10 domain by Nolen Royalty. About 400k visitors made 650M+ checks in two weeks, hiding binary messages and animations. The backend was rewritten from Python to Go under load. It shut after two weeks and never made money [72].
  **Lesson:** A shared, live, collective state beats a single-player joke.
- **neal.fun (2017–).** Neal Agarwal's toys (Spend Bill Gates' Money, The Password Game, Infinite Craft) earned enough ad revenue to be his full-time living by graduation [73].
  **Lesson:** A portfolio of one-joke toys under one name compounds, and display ads can fund it.
- **Dumb Starbucks (February 2014).** Nathan Fielder's "parody" coffee shop gave away coffee under a fair-use FAQ. It drew hour-long lines and global press, and health officials shut it within days [74].
  **Lesson:** A parody that exists in the real world is press catnip. Permits are the real risk.
- **Also:** The Million Dollar Homepage (§3) is the classic one-joke site that made money, and every CAH stunt was its own one-page site.

## 8. Patterns across the winners

1. **The purchase is the joke.** $5 for nothing, $6 for poop, $1 a pixel. Buyers get a story, so they tell it.
2. **Commit to the bit.** The poop ships, the hole gets dug, the soul contract has clauses. Being real is what makes it news.
3. **Transparency.** CAH's product page said plainly that the box contained actual feces [4]. Winners never trick the buyer; losers (Wish, scam boxes, deceptive AI) do.
4. **Scarcity with a start time.** 30,000 boxes, 100 boards, 666 pairs, noon ET. The sell-out is the second headline.
5. **A printable number, ideally live.** $71,145; $100,573; $1,037,100; 50.9bn XP; 650M checks.
6. **A goodwill kicker.** Heifer International, $250k to charities, $10M to the planet, paid days off. It disarms "cash grab".
7. **A cultural moment.** Black Friday, the Super Bowl, the NBA Finals, a celebrity's post.
8. **Participation that recruits.** Dollars buy digging seconds, XP revives Duo, fans become Old Spice's cast.
9. **Cheap and fast beats polished.** $4,500 (Dollar Shave Club), ~$2,000 (Kalshi), $10 (One Million Checkboxes).
10. **Tradition.** CAH's 13 years and REI's permanent closure are fixed dates on the press calendar.
11. **A consistent character.** Duo, the Old Spice Guy, Ryanair's plane, Liquid Death's metal voice.

**Anti-patterns:**
- Deceiving people.
- AI as prestige cosplay.
- Disappointment as the product.
- Real dark patterns in real commerce.
- A persona betrayed by the business behind it.

## 9. What this means for Order That Shit

### Implications

1. **The one real thing is the news.** On a site that fakes everything, a *real* number (euros raised, units shipped, authorizations issued) is what journalists can print. Label it: "Real number. For once."
2. **One bit, one URL, one number.** Give the stunt its own path and OG card, reusing the pipeline that already gives every Shit Finder verdict its own preview card. This is CAH's microsite format.
3. **Build the counter first.** The site is static, so the enabling piece is a tiny server-side counter (e.g., a Worker plus a Durable Object or KV). Count buckets only (the deliberation chip, a category picked client-side) and never store typed text.
4. **Keep visitors in on the joke.** Say exactly what they get. For anything random, publish the pool and the odds; China's rules and the ASA rulings point the same way. Make refunds one click.
5. **Wall off the parody dark patterns.** The ups-only stock counter, endless flash sale and fake purchase toasts are fine as satire, but they are what the EU pursued Temu for. On any page that takes real money, every claim must be literally true.
6. **Budget it as marketing.** CAH kept ~$6k of $180k and openly expects to lose money on the 99% sale. Set a hard cap you're happy to lose. Announce any goodwill kicker up front and publish the receipts afterwards.
7. **For the €1 mystery order, prefer "the product is the joke" to "random cheap stuff".** Random cheap items risk Wish-style disappointment and look like the scam ads. If you go random, make it a capped, transparent, loss-leading drop, not a business.
8. **Design for the share moment.** The 1080×1920 Order Authorization is right. Add a serial number and "Black Friday 2026 Edition" so it becomes collectible.
9. **AI video: absurd, labeled, a recurring cast.** Linda, Marcus and Gary are the right register. Add an "AI-generated, obviously" tag. No photoreal "real customer" reviews and no sentimental prestige pastiche. The legal brief covers EU AI Act transparency rules.
10. **Personalize with templates.** Old Spice made ~186 replies by hand. Pre-rendered character clips with personalized text or voice lines are cheaper and easier to moderate than generating a video per user. Many small clips beat one big film, because formats saturate in weeks.
11. **Timing and press.** Pre-announce a fixed drop time that catches the EU afternoon and the US morning (15:00 CET = 09:00 ET). Pitch the "best Black Friday stunt" round-ups that covered CAH every year [3][4][5][6][9].
12. **Plan the second story and the sequel.** Publish a recap with numbers on Giving Tuesday (December 1, 2026), as CAH did with the $71,145. Name the event so it can come back every year.

### Black Friday 2026 stunt concepts

Costs are rough planning estimates, not quotes; check the economics and AI-tooling briefs.

**A. "Order ONE Thing Friday" (flagship)**
- **Stunt:**
  - From 00:00 CET on November 27, the site goes "100% off everything (everything is nothing)". The fake tiers' prices rise every hour: "the only Black Friday deal that gets worse".
  - The real pitch: "Black Friday is for stuff you don't want. Today we authorize ONE thing you do."
  - Each visitor gets a numbered Black Friday Authorization (#000001…).
  - A real, labeled counter tracks "Things finally ordered" and "Months of overthinking cured".
  - On Giving Tuesday, publish "The Overthinking Report 2026".
- **Why:**
  - Permission is the one thing the site genuinely gives people.
  - Raising prices instead of cutting them follows CAH 2013 and Allbirds.
  - A public number worked for the Million Dollar Homepage and Duolingo's XP.
  - It borrows the "buy less but better" credibility of Patagonia and Deciem without preaching.
  - The recap is a second news cycle, as CAH's was in 2015.
- **Cost:** 2–4 dev days, ~€5–20/month for the counter, €0 media.
- **Risk:** Low.
  - Hide the counter until it passes a threshold.
  - The numbers must be real.
  - The "ONE thing" framing defends against reading as pro-consumption.

**B. "Nothing. €1." (real-money doorbuster, charity kicker)**
- **Stunt:** For 24 hours, pay €1 or more for literally nothing. All net proceeds go to a named charity (anti-waste or repair fits the message). There's a live euro counter, a numbered "Certificate of Having Ordered Nothing", and a limit of one per person.
- **Why:**
  - CAH 2015 raised $71,145 from 11,248 people for nothing.
  - Patagonia 2016 did 5× its expectations when proceeds went to a cause.
  - Donations defused CAH's "cash grab" critique.
- **Cost:** 1–2 dev days. Fixed card fees eat much of €1, so consider a €2 floor or pay-what-you-want. A charity agreement is needed.
- **Risk:**
  - Tax and charity-promotion rules (legal brief).
  - €1 checkouts attract card-testing fraud; add bot protection.
  - Confused buyers will ask for refunds, as CAH's did; make refunds instant.
  - Low turnout.

**C. "That Shit™ — Black Friday Limited Edition" (monetization test)**
- **Stunt:**
  - A numbered run of 1,000–2,026 boxes of *that shit*: one absurd, safe, cheap object (Pet Rock-style), a care manual and a printed Certificate.
  - Optional disclosed variants, such as a 1-in-100 "golden" edition (after Labubu's secret figures and the Diamond Potato).
  - Drop at 15:00 CET, one per customer, with a CAPTCHA or lottery against bots, as CAH did in 2025.
  - Price it to cover the landed cost, or at €1 as a capped loss-leader.
  - Ship from an EU fulfiller before Christmas, with an unboxing prompt.
- **Why:**
  - Pet Rock (~1.5M) and Bullshit (30k in ~30 min) sold because the product was the joke.
  - Liquid Death's boards and the Diamond Potato (sold out in under an hour, resold ~6×) show capped drops sell out.
  - Labubu shows the reveal is the content; fukubukuro shows a ritual plus a value floor makes it a treat.
  - It's the precedent-backed version of the €1 mystery order: a known, funny object avoids Wish-style disappointment and the scam pattern.
- **Cost:** Very roughly €7–16 per unit landed for a small EU run (get quotes), so €7–16k exposure per 1,000 units. Near-zero net if priced to cover (CAH kept ~3%).
- **Risk:**
  - Eight weeks is tight: lock a supplier by ~October 20.
  - EU consumer and product-safety duties: the 14-day withdrawal right, GPSR, packaging (legal brief).
  - Botched fulfilment turns the joke into complaints.
  - Random contents add odds disclosure, rules on minors and scam optics.

**D. "The Infomercial Never Ends — Live" (AI video + participation)**
- **Stunt:** A Black Friday livestream, the "live edition" of the 2h14m infomercial.
  - Viewers submit their "thing", and the team posts short personalized AI replies from Linda, Marcus, Gary or the CEO.
  - Each authorization issued adds 10 seconds to the stream, which ends when the clock runs out.
  - The best replies become Shorts and Reels for a week.
- **Why:**
  - It combines Old Spice's personalized Responses, CAH's Holiday Hole ($100,573 buying seconds of digging), Duolingo's collective XP goal and One Million Checkboxes' shared state.
  - AI makes the personalization cheap.
  - Audiences accept labeled absurdity: 73% are fine with disclosure (IAB), and 76% want to know (Pew).
- **Cost:** Roughly €500–3,000: AI generation for a few hundred short clips (per-clip price depends on the model; see the tooling brief) plus 1–2 moderators for 12–24 hours.
- **Risk:**
  - User text in public video: pre-filter it and have a human approve.
  - Render latency: use templates and overlays.
  - A small live audience: the clips are the real payload anyway.
  - "AI slop" backlash: stay obviously comedic, and never make photoreal fake customers.

**E. "Closed for Black Friday (to Order That Shit)" (cheap teaser, #OptOutside parody)**
- **Stunt:**
  - At 00:00 CET the site shows a "Closed" page: the whole staff has the day off to finally order that shit.
  - Four AI clips show Linda, Marcus, Gary and the CEO each ordering their own thing.
  - It reopens at 15:00 CET with the main stunt. On Cyber Monday: "Still closed. Recovering."
- **Why:**
  - REI proved "closed" can be the stunt; it's now in year 10.
  - Deciem and Freitag closed too.
  - A parody of a known ritual reads instantly and keeps the cast central.
- **Cost:** 4–6 clips and a page variant, about a day of work; tens to low hundreds of euros.
- **Risk:** Low. Keep the authorization flow reachable so first-time visitors don't bounce.

**Suggested combination:**
1. E as the teaser (00:00–15:00 CET).
2. A all weekend.
3. One real-money element: B (simplest) or C (only with a supplier confirmed by ~October 20).
4. D if the AI budget and two people are available.
5. The recap on Giving Tuesday.
