# 06: The €1 mystery order, and what to sell instead

*Compiled 2026-09-29 by a research agent (sources checked 29–30 Sept 2026). **This is not legal, tax or financial advice.** It maps the ground so you can take the right questions to a Portuguese lawyer and a certified accountant (contabilista certificado). "Estimate" marks an assumption, not a quote. [n] points to the source list at the end.*

---

## TL;DR

**Verdict: don't launch the €1 dropshipped mystery order**, neither for Black Friday 2026 nor later in that form. Any one of these four reasons is enough:

1. **Every order loses about €6–8.**
   - Since 1 July 2026, low-value parcels from outside the EU pay a flat **€3 customs duty per item line** [28].
   - The Commission adopted a **€2 per-item EU handling fee** on 21 Sept 2026. It is still subject to Parliament and Council scrutiny and should apply from about November [29].
   - With card fees, shipping and support on top, a €1 order costs about €8.60 to fulfil.
   - A single China-shipped item breaks even at **about €11 incl. VAT (€8–13)**. 10,000 orders would lose about €78,000.
2. **Sold as "a gambling thing", it is probably illegal for a company in Portugal.**
   - A paid draw where your hope of something better depends on luck is a *modalidade afim* of gambling, in the same family as a *tômbola*.
   - For-profit entities may not run one at all (DL 422/89, art. 161) [1].
   - Stripe prohibits paid entry for a chance at a prize, and PayPal requires pre-approval [32][33].
3. **The supply side is closed or toxic.**
   - Temu's EU terms allow personal, non-commercial use only [34].
   - Cheap marketplace goods are what regulators are acting on: Temu was fined €200m under the DSA on 28 May 2026 over unsafe baby toys and chargers [25], and 18 of 19 toys bought on Temu failed EU rules in one test [22].
   - As a dropshipper you are probably their **importer**, with the product-safety and liability duties that come with it [21].
4. **Consumer law doesn't shrink for €1.** Every order still carries a 14-day withdrawal right, the new **withdrawal button (since 19 June 2026)**, Portugal's **3-year guarantee**, an invoice and a complaints book [15–17].

**What to do instead, ranked:**
1. A capped, mailed **"Certificate of Having Ordered That Shit — Physical Edition"** with one random sticker inside. Every variant has the same value and all are shown before purchase. About €1.85–2.30 margin at €5.99; launchable in about 3 weeks.
2. A free **"Shit Roulette"** that reveals real, curated cheap products through disclosed affiliate links. No gambling risk and little revenue, but it spreads well.
3. **Print-on-demand merch** and/or a **€4.99 personalised AI testimonial**, using the video pipeline the site already has.

In every option the surprise is *what* you get, never *how much it's worth*.

## 1. Is it gambling?

### 1.1 The test

European gambling laws generally catch schemes with all three of:
- **consideration**: you pay;
- **chance**: you don't control the outcome;
- **a prize**: something of value you hope to win.

Remove any one of the three and the scheme isn't gambling. A €1 mystery order clearly has the first two. So the whole question is whether the item is a "prize" or simply the goods you bought.

### 1.2 Portugal: DL 422/89 (the gaming law) and the online regime

I read the consolidated text published by SRIJ, Portugal's gambling regulator [1].

**Games of fortune or chance** (*jogos de fortuna ou azar*)
- These are games whose result rests exclusively or fundamentally on luck (art. 1).
- On land they are allowed only in casinos inside the legal gaming zones (art. 3).
- Running one anywhere else is a **crime**: up to 2 years' prison and a fine of up to 200 days (art. 108).
- Online, only SRIJ-licensed games under the online gambling regime (RJO, DL 66/2015) are allowed. SRIJ says unregulated online games and bets are prohibited [3].

**Related modalities** (*modalidades afins*, art. 159)
These are offers to the public where the hope of gain rests on luck (with or without skill) and the prizes have a predetermined economic value. The law names raffles (*rifas*), *tômbolas*, draws (*sorteios*), advertising contests (*concursos publicitários*), quizzes and pastimes.
- They need authorisation from the **mayor (Presidente da Câmara)** of the municipality concerned, or of the operator's seat if they reach further (art. 160). This competence moved to the municipalities under DL 98/2018 [1][2].
- **For-profit entities may not run them** (art. 161(1)). The only exceptions are media quizzes and pastimes, and **advertising contests that promote goods or services**.
- Even those exceptions may cost the player nothing beyond normal postage or telecoms, a newspaper's cover price, or the normal price of the promoted product (art. 161(2)).
- They **may not use casino themes**: poker, fruit, bells, **roulette**, dice, bingo, lotteries (art. 161(3)).
- A breach is a serious economic offence (*contraordenação económica grave*) under the economic-offences regime (RJCE). Proceeds can be seized and the activity banned for up to 6 months (art. 163). SRIJ acts as technical adviser to the enforcers (art. 164).

**A telling analogy.** Vending machines, where the price must match the goods' commercial value, may not hand out any prize (art. 162(2)). That is the spirit a regulator would bring to a "pay €1, get something random" machine.

**No public position.** I found no SRIJ statement on loot boxes or physical mystery boxes. A 2021 academic review calls the municipal regime "a regime of uncertainty" [2].

### 1.3 How your idea would probably be classified

| Design | What the buyer gets | Likely treatment in Portugal | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. "Lucky dip"** | A random item for €1, some worth far more ("1 in 500 gets AirPods"), sold with "win / jackpot / rare" language or a slot or roulette screen | A *modalidade afim* resembling a tômbola, or even a game of chance. A company may not run it, the roulette theme is banned, and payment processors prohibit it. | **Red** |

The owner's own framing, "make it like a gambling thing", is the core problem. The more it looks like gambling (casino visuals, chase items, "you won!"), the more likely a regulator treats it as gambling.

### 1.4 EU context

There is no EU gambling law, so each country applies its own. EU consumer law is meanwhile moving against gambling-like mechanics.

**National positions**
- **Belgium (April 2018):** the Gaming Commission ruled paid loot boxes in Overwatch, FIFA 18 and CS:GO were illegal games of chance [4].
- **Netherlands:** the gambling regulator (Kansspelautoriteit) treated tradeable loot boxes as gambling in 2018 [6]. On 9 March 2022 the Council of State annulled its penalty on EA's FIFA packs, finding them part of a larger game of skill (ECLI:NL:RVS:2022:690) [5].
- **Spain:** a bill protecting minors online would ban minors' access to random-reward mechanics. It was still in committee in Congress on 29 Sept 2026 [7].
- **Germany:** deals with loot boxes through youth protection and age ratings rather than gambling law (not re-verified).
- **France:** bans lotteries under a broad definition, a paid hope of gain that depends even partly on chance (Code de la sécurité intérieure, L.320-1; not re-verified). **Italy** regulates prize promotions under DPR 430/2001 (not re-verified). Every extra country is another legal analysis, so launch in Portugal first.

**EU level**
- **European Parliament (18 Jan 2023):** a resolution, adopted 577–66–15, asked the Commission to assess loot boxes and legislate if needed [8].
- **CPC Network** (the national consumer authorities acting together):
  - March 2024: non-binding principles on in-game currencies, requiring real-money prices, no hidden costs, respect for withdrawal rights and protection of children [9].
  - November 2024: action against Temu for fake discounts, false urgency and **forcing users to play a "spin the fortune wheel" game** to enter the marketplace [10].
  - May 2025: action against Shein on similar grounds [10].
- **Digital Fairness Act:**
  - its consultation (17 July–24 Oct 2025) covered virtual currencies and loot boxes [12];
  - as of 1 Aug 2026 there was **still no Commission proposal**, which is scheduled for Q4 2026 [11];
  - nothing from it applies on Black Friday 2026, but the direction is clear: disclosed odds, limits for minors, fewer dark patterns.

### 1.5 Reference points worth copying

- **UK Advertising Standards Authority (ASA):**
  - March 2024: upheld three complaints against game ads that hid loot boxes;
  - November 2025: ruled that an in-app "spin" in F1 Clash (Hutch Games) was misleading because it didn't make clear the prizes weren't equally likely [13];
  - the UK still doesn't classify loot boxes or mystery boxes as gambling.
- **China's market regulator (SAMR), blind-box guidelines of June 2023:**
  - no sales to children under 8;
  - disclosure of the contents' value, the draw rules and the probabilities;
  - banned categories such as medicine, medical devices, live animals and flammables;
  - spending caps encouraged [14].

**Checklist for any random element you ship:**
- publish the whole pool with each item's value;
- use equal odds, or publish them;
- never say "win", "jackpot" or "rare";
- no casino visuals on anything paid;
- 18+ only, with a per-person cap;
- nothing aimed at children.

## 2. Consumer law (applies to anything that takes money)

| Rule | What it means for a mystery order |
|---|---|
| **Pre-contract information**: Consumer Rights Directive (CRD) 2011/83/EU, art. 6; Portugal DL 24/2014 [15] | You must state the goods' "main characteristics". For random contents that means publishing the pool (photo, description and value of each possible item), the total price with delivery, the delivery time, the country it ships from and who pays for returns [21]. |
| **Order button** (CRD art. 8(2)) | The button must make clear that ordering means paying. "ORDER THAT SHIT" alone on a real payment button is risky, so add plain text. |
| **14-day withdrawal** (CRD arts. 9–16) | There is **no exception for surprise goods**. Refund within 14 days, including standard outbound delivery. The buyer pays for returns only if you said so in advance. Two exceptions can help: **clearly personalised goods** (e.g. a certificate with the buyer's name printed on it), and digital content supplied immediately with the buyer's express consent and acknowledgement. |
| **Withdrawal button**: Directive (EU) 2023/2673, adding CRD art. 11a | Mandatory **since 19 June 2026** [16]. Portugal was late to transpose it and its current status is unverified. Build it anyway. |
| **Legal guarantee**: DL 84/2021 | **3 years** for goods in Portugal [17]. |
| **Delivery** (CRD art. 18) | Deliver within 30 days unless a date was agreed, so pre-orders need a stated date. |
| **Unfair practices**: UCPD 2005/29/EC (PT DL 57/2008); Omnibus Directive 2019/2161 (PT Lei 10/2023) [18] | Blacklisted: false limited-time claims (Annex I, point 7); a false impression of having won a prize (point 31); fake reviews or endorsements (points 23b–c). **Warning specific to this site.** The parody runs a never-ending flash sale, a stock counter that only goes up, fake purchase toasts, an exit-intent modal and fake testimonials.
- That is harmless while nothing is for sale.
- On a page that takes real money, the same elements become textbook unfair practices, the very ones the CPC flagged at Temu and Shein [10].
- Keep the joke machinery on the joke pages. Make any real checkout plain and visibly separate, and label the testimonials as fiction.

**Two other regimes:**
- **Digital Services Act (DSA):** its marketplace duties don't apply to a shop selling only its own goods.
- **AI Act art. 50:** requires disclosure of AI-generated video from 2 Aug 2026. Check whether the 2025–26 "digital omnibus" moved any dates.

## 3. Product safety and liability

**GPSR** (Regulation (EU) 2023/988, applies since 13 Dec 2024) [20]
- Consumer products must be safe, and an **EU-established economic operator** must be responsible for each one (art. 16).
- Online listings must show the manufacturer's name and postal and email address, the EU responsible person if the manufacturer is outside the EU, product identification with a picture, and warnings in the buyer's language (art. 19).
- The Commission's 2022 Blue Guide treats an offer targeted at EU consumers, or fulfilled to them, as placing the product on the EU market [39].

**Your role as a dropshipper**
- Buy outside the EU and sell inside it, and **you may be the importer** (Dutch Chamber of Commerce guidance, updated 8 Sept 2026 [21]). Importers must check conformity, get the technical file, put their name and address on the product, log complaints and act on dangerous products.
- Sell under your own brand and you are the **manufacturer**. For a random pool of sub-€1 SKUs from unknown factories, none of this is manageable.

**Sector rules on top**
- Toys (Directive 2009/48/EC, CE marking).
- Electrical goods: LVD, EMC and RED directives, RoHS, plus producer registration for WEEE and batteries.
- Cosmetics (Regulation 1223/2009: responsible person and CPNP notification).
- Food-contact items (Regulation 1935/2004).
- Jewellery (REACH limits on nickel, lead and cadmium).

Excluding these categories wipes out most of the "cheap shit" pool.

**Evidence the pool is contaminated**
- Toy Industries of Europe (TIE) bought 19 toys on Temu at the end of 2023. **18 failed**, and none named an EU responsible operator [22].
- Belgian consumer group Test Aankoop (3 Dec 2025) found **14 of 20** toys from Temu, Shein, Wish, Amazon and others non-compliant. It cites a 2025 TIE test where 86% failed [23].
- The Commission issued preliminary DSA findings against Temu on 28 July 2025, based on mystery shopping of baby toys and small electronics [24].
- It followed with a **€200m fine on 28 May 2026**, the largest DSA fine so far, over failing chargers and baby toys with excess chemicals or small parts [25].
- It opened formal DSA proceedings against Shein on 17 Feb 2026, including over illegal products [26].

**Product liability**
- Today: the 1985 regime (in Portugal, DL 383/89), under which the EU importer is liable like the producer.
- For products placed on the market **after 9 Dec 2026**, the new Product Liability Directive (2024/2853) applies [27]:
  - if the maker is outside the EU, the importer, authorised representative or fulfilment provider is liable;
  - a distributor becomes liable if it can't name any of them within a month;
  - damages now include psychological harm and data loss.
- Nobody will cheaply insure a random pool of goods from China.

**Extended producer responsibility (EPR).** Packaging placed on the Portuguese market is covered by EPR, and the EU Packaging Regulation 2025/40 applies from 12 Aug 2026 (not re-verified). Ask the accountant about registration at small volumes.

## 4. Customs and VAT: what changed in 2026

**IOSS (since July 2021).** For consignments up to €150, the seller or marketplace collects VAT at checkout and the import itself is VAT-free.
- Without IOSS, the buyer pays at the door.
- CTT charges **€7 + VAT** to clear a non-IOSS purchase under €150 (page updated 24 June 2026) [31]. On a €1 item, that alone kills the business.

**The €3 duty** (Council Regulation (EU) 2026/382; 1 July 2026 to 1 July 2028) [28]
- The €150 duty relief is gone.
- Every **B2C distance sale** in a consignment up to €150, whether or not VAT goes through IOSS, pays **€3 per "item"**.
- An item is one customs-declaration line (same tariff classification, description and origin), however many units it covers. The Commission's example: 5 identical T-shirts cost €3; 3 T-shirts plus a watch cost €6.
- Platforms build it into their prices.
- The Commission had to assess by 1 Oct 2026 whether to widen the duty to all low-value goods.
- Stock already in free circulation in the EU is unaffected. Selling out of a customs warehouse does *not* escape the duty (Commission guidance, 2 June 2026) [28].

**The €2 EU handling fee** [29]
- It sits in the new Union Customs Code, Regulation (EU) 2026/2108, published 19 Sept 2026.
- Delegated Regulation C(2026) 6694 of 21 Sept 2026 sets it at **€2 per item** for goods imported through distance sales.
- It applies 10 days after publication in the Official Journal, which follows the Parliament and Council objection period. That is expected **around November 2026**, so treat it as live for Black Friday.

**National fees** (secondary sources, verify) [30]
- France: €2 per item line since 1 March 2026.
- Romania: 25 RON per parcel since January 2026.
- Italy: its €2 fee has been postponed again.
- Portugal: no national fee found.

**Your own VAT**
- Rates: **23%** on the mainland, 22% in Madeira, 16% in the Azores.
- Once cross-border consumer sales within the EU exceed **€10,000** a year, you charge destination VAT and file through the One-Stop Shop (OSS).
- The small-business exemption (art. 53 of the VAT Code, CIVA; threshold about €15,000 from 2025) has historically excluded import/export activity, so confirm you qualify.
- **Marketplace dropshipping risks paying VAT twice.** The marketplace charges IOSS VAT on its sale to you, you charge 23% on your own sale, and the first may not be deductible.
- Portugal expects **an invoice per sale**, normally issued through tax-authority-certified software. At €1 an order, invoicing cost and volume matter.

## 5. Payment processors

**Stripe** [32]
- Its restricted list (updated 22 Sept 2026) prohibits "games of chance … with a monetary or material prize", lotteries, and **entry-fee payments that promise the entrant a prize of value**.
- A €1 box sold as a gamble falls squarely inside that. If Stripe agrees, the likely result is account closure and held funds.
- A transparent random sale isn't named on the list, but Stripe decides.
- Portugal pricing: **1.5% + €0.25** on standard EEA cards, 2.5% + €0.25 on UK cards, 3.15% + €0.25 on international cards, +2% for currency conversion, and **€20 per dispute**.
- MB WAY is supported for Portuguese accounts, from €0.50.

**PayPal** [33]
- Its acceptable use policy (17 July 2023) requires pre-approval for gambling and "any other activity with an entry fee and a prize".
- Portugal fees (effective 15 July 2026): **2.90% + €0.35**; micropayments at 5% + a fixed fee, on application; €16 chargeback fee; €14 dispute fee.

**The maths on €1**
- Stripe keeps €0.27 (27%); PayPal keeps €0.38.
- One €20 dispute costs the gross revenue of 20 orders.
- Mystery goods attract "not as described" claims, and the card networks police dispute ratios.
- After VAT and card fees, a €1 sale leaves €0.54. A 1% dispute rate alone eats €0.21 of that.
- Card fees are 27% of the price at €1, about 10% at €3 and about 6.5% at €5.

- Sending names, addresses and phone numbers to suppliers in China is a transfer to a country without an EU adequacy decision.

**a. Shit Roulette**
- For Portuguese traffic, join **Amazon.es Associates**. Its policies (updated 14 Apr 2026) require [36]:
  - Amazon's prescribed disclosure sentence ("En calidad de Afiliado de Amazon…");
  - prices shown only via Amazon's product API with a timestamp, or not at all;
  - **no incentives or rewards for clicks or purchases**;
  - no redirect or cloaked links;
  - no obscene content.
- A profane domain might still be rejected, so keep a second affiliate network in reserve.
- Don't force the spin (the CPC flagged Temu's mandatory wheel [10]) and never attach a prize to it (§1.3). Label affiliate links as advertising.

**b. POD merch.** Printful has no monthly fee [37], and its API is easy to call from a Worker.

**c. Paid digital product**
- Digital content loses the withdrawal right only with the buyer's express consent and acknowledgement, plus a confirmation afterwards (CRD art. 16(m)).
- VAT follows the customer's country once EU cross-border sales pass €10,000, filed through OSS.
- A merchant of record handles that VAT for you: Paddle or Lemon Squeezy at 5% + $0.50, or Stripe Managed Payments at 3.5% on top of card fees (digital goods only) [32][38]. Paddle asks sellers of products under $10 to contact them for custom pricing.
- **At €1–3, fixed fees and compute eat the price. €4.99 is the floor for video.**
- Use fictional personas only, with a visible "AI-generated" label.

**d. Physical drop and charity stunt**
- The certificate drop is the answer to "surprise without gambling": equal-value sticker variants, all shown before purchase.
- For "€1 for nothing", link to the charity's own MB WAY or donation page. Collecting €1 yourself costs 27% in fees and makes you answerable for the donation claim.

**e. Sponsored ads and waitlist.** Sponsored parody ads are the only high-margin option; the Portuguese advertising code (Código da Publicidade) requires ads to be identifiable as ads. A waitlist validates the 2027 box before you buy any stock.

### Before Black Friday (8 weeks)

4. **Weeks 3–5:** add POD merch if you have the capacity.

   - run a joke like "prices go *up* today" rather than a fake discount (the 30-day prior-price rule applies to any reduction);
   - for "€1 for nothing", point to a charity's own page.

### Later (2027)

- A €12.99–14.99 box of EU-stocked, documented items.
- Sponsored parody ads.
- AI video at €4.99 or more, once moderation is in place.
- Revisit gamified mechanics once the Digital Fairness Act text is out.

### Avoid

- Per-order imports from China.
- Temu as a supplier.
- Any *paid* mechanic where luck decides how much value you get.
- Casino visuals on paid flows.
- Toys, electronics, cosmetics and jewellery.
- Fake urgency, fake stock, toasts or fake reviews near a real checkout.

### Risk register

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| A paid random mechanic is classed as a *modalidade afim* or game of chance | Low for design C; low–medium for B; **high for A** | High (economic offence; a crime if a game of chance; payment processor ban) | Equal-value pool shown in full, no "win" wording or casino UI on paid flows, 18+, lawyer's sign-off |
| Stripe or PayPal freezes or closes the account | Medium | High | Describe the business accurately, avoid gambling wording, keep disputes low, use a clear statement descriptor |
| Unsafe product or liability claim | High for a marketplace pool; low for paper and textiles | High | Only your own-spec, EU-made, documented products |
| Duties and fees turn orders into losses | Certain for China dropshipping | High | Ship from Portugal |

### Questions for a Portuguese lawyer

1. Is a paid "random item from a published pool of equal-value items" a generic sale (Civil Code arts. 400/539) or a *modalidade afim* (DL 422/89, art. 159)? Does disclosing equal values and odds remove the "hope of gain"? Do random sticker variants change the answer?
2. Could a free roulette that awards nothing raise any issue under art. 161(3)? If we ever add a prize, which Câmara authorises it, on what conditions, and is stamp duty due on prizes?
3. Consumer set-up:
   - how to present pre-contract information for random variants;
   - whether a personalised certificate qualifies for the withdrawal exception;
   - whether Portugal has transposed the withdrawal button yet;
   - which ADR body to name.
4. Which parody elements (toasts, counters, testimonials, "flash sale") must go, or be labelled, on pages that sell?
5. GPSR: for our own-brand paper certificate and sticker, are we the "manufacturer", and what labelling and documentation is the minimum?
6. AI videos: what does AI Act art. 50 require us to disclose? What personality-rights and defamation risks do we carry, and what moderation duties?
7. Can "Order That Shit" be registered as a trademark (INPI/EUIPO), given the refusal grounds for marks contrary to morality?

### Questions for an accountant

1. Sole trader or single-member company, which activity (CAE) codes, whether art. 53 CIVA works alongside EU sales, and when OSS kicks in.
2. Certified invoicing: one invoice per Stripe payment? Are simplified invoices allowed? How are ATCUD codes handled?
3. VAT on digital sales to EU consumers (OSS or a merchant of record?) and on Printful invoices (reverse charge?).
4. Packaging EPR registration at small volumes.
5. The charity stunt: should the charity collect directly, and how is anything we pass on taxed?
