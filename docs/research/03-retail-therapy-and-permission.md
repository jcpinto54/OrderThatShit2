# 03: Retail therapy, permission, and the pleasure of "it's on its way"

*Compiled 2026-09-29 by a research agent; verify before quoting publicly. Scope: the consumer psychology of buying as mood repair, self-permission, anticipation, surprise, post-purchase feelings and harm. The legal and unit-economics case for the mystery order is in brief 06; virality, humour, platforms and case studies are covered by the other briefs. Citations were checked against publisher pages, PubMed, Crossref or author-posted PDFs; anything weaker is labelled.*

---

## TL;DR

1. **The active ingredient in retail therapy is choosing, not owning.** In the one clean experimental series (Rick, Pereira & Burson 2014), making shopping choices reduced lingering sadness more than browsing did, **even when the choices were hypothetical**, because choosing restores a sense of control. It did nothing for anger. A free, fake order can plausibly deliver a real, if small, lift. **[Suggestive]**
2. **Guilt is the barrier, so people go looking for a reason.** Effort ("I worked hard"), excellence (even fake praise), past restraint and a practical alibi all make indulging more likely. Mentioning the money cancels the effect. **[Robust as a pattern]**
3. **Reasons change the forecast, not the experience.** People *expect* to feel guiltier when they indulge without a reason, but in the moment they don't (Xu & Schwarz 2009). A satirical "Official Justification™" therefore works where guilt lives: at the click.
4. **Much of the pleasure is in wanting and waiting.** Anticipation is more intense than memory, and for materialists desire peaks *before* purchase. "It's on its way" is prime emotional and comedic real estate. **[Robust / Suggestive]**
5. **A mystery works best as "certainly good, uncertain which".** Not knowing *which* nice thing is coming makes good moods last longer, although people predict the opposite. Uncertainty about *whether* it's good, or *when* it comes, doesn't help. **[Suggestive, several labs]**
6. **Finality helps.** Choosing makes you like what you chose (artifact-free meta-analysis, d ≈ 0.40), reversible decisions satisfy less, and closure rituals help. The Certificate is psychologically well placed. **[Robust / Suggestive]**
7. **Every lever cuts both ways.** Sad people overpay, humour lowers resistance, uncertainty increases investment, and about 5% of adults buy compulsively. Keep the feel-good core free, and cap and de-gamify anything that costs money. **[Robust]**

**Evidence tags:**
- **[Robust]**: replicated, meta-analysed, or found by many independent studies.
- **[Suggestive]**: a few studies, small samples or one lab; plausible but unproven.
- **[Lore/cultural]**: practitioner belief, vendor surveys or memes. It tells you what people say and do, not why.
- **[Not robust]**: large replications failed.

## 1. Does ordering something actually make you feel better?

### 1.1 The best experiment: choosing restores control

Rick, Pereira & Burson (2014) ran three experiments:

- **Exp. 1 (100 online adults, hypothetical purchases).** Everyone first watched a sad film clip.
  - "Choosers" picked 4 of 12 products they would *hypothetically* buy with $100.
  - "Browsers" picked the 4 products most useful for travel.
  - Both tasks took about 60 seconds.
  - Residual sadness (0–100 scale, final minus baseline) was **2.9 for choosers vs 8.1 for browsers**.
  - In a pre-test, 79% said they felt more in control while choosing; 2% said so while browsing.
- **Exp. 2 (147 students, real $5 purchase).** Everyone got what they chose. Those who believed a computer *might* have overridden their choice stayed sadder (+3.7 vs −2.3). Choosing had no effect on anger.
- **Exp. 3 (301 students).** Recalling a moment of personal control reduced residual sadness (2.9 vs 12.1), but not anger.

**Caveats:**
- My back-of-envelope effect sizes from the reported t-tests are d ≈ 0.4–0.6, with roughly 35–50 people per cell.
- I found no direct replication.
- The authors raise the open question themselves: does the control you gain now cost you control later through debt?
- Press coverage says shopping is "40 times" better at restoring control and leaves people "three times less sad". Those figures appear to be the pre-test ratio (79% vs 2%) and the Exp. 1 ratio (8.1 vs 2.9). Don't quote them.

**[Suggestive]**

### 1.2 Field and diary evidence

- **Atalay & Meloy (2011):**
  - A bad mood led to more unplanned treats for oneself.
  - People used treats strategically, and held back when restraint would also lift their mood.
  - The mood gains lasted, and mood-repair purchases were not followed by regret or guilt.
  - The BPS Research Digest summary of the diary study: 62% of these purchases were for low mood and 28% for celebration. Mood-repair treats cost about half as much, and only one participant said they would return one.
  - Self-report data from mostly student samples. **[Suggestive]**
- **Mick & DeMoss (1990):** interviews show that self-gifts (as reward or as therapy) are usually premeditated, tied to context and symbolic: a message to yourself. **[Qualitative]**
- **Kacen & Lee (2002):** across five countries, impulse buying varied with individualism vs collectivism. Expect reactions to differ across markets. **[Suggestive]**

### 1.3 Why sad people buy, and why that's risky

- **People indulge because they expect it to fix their mood.** When told their bad mood was "frozen" (unchangeable), people stopped reaching for snacks, instant gratification and procrastination (Tice, Bratslavsky & Baumeister 2001). **[Suggestive: older single-lab paradigm]**
- **Sadness raises what people will pay** (Lerner, Small & Loewenstein 2004; Cryder et al. 2008). A replication found it again, even when participants had another way to compensate; the pooled estimate was d = 0.43 (Garg, Williams & Lerner 2018). **[Robust]** This is the harm side of retail therapy.
- **Buying can patch a bruised self-image.** The Compensatory Consumer Behavior Model lists five strategies: direct resolution, symbolic self-completion, dissociation, escapism and fluid compensation (Mandel et al. 2017).
  - But buying something that *symbolises the very thing you feel bad about* can trigger rumination and weaken later self-control (Lisjak et al. 2015).
  - The review notes that validation from other people seems to defuse this. **[Suggestive]**

### 1.4 When it backfires

It backfires with anger, which choosing doesn't fix, with later debt, and with compulsive buyers (section 7). Retail therapy also has a PR problem. When Rick et al. asked 100 adults for the first word "retail therapy" brought to mind, negative answers ("nonsense", "debt") beat positive ones by 19% to 8%.

## 2. Permission: how people let themselves buy

- **Hedonic purchases need a reason; practical ones don't.**
  - Seen alone, a fun item is rated higher than a practical one. Seen side by side, people pick the practical one.
  - People will pay in *time* (hunting for bargains) for fun things and in *money* for practical things (Okada 2005).
  - Fun-vs-function trade-offs trigger guilt and anxiety alongside excitement (Chitturi, Raghunathan & Mahajan 2007).

  **[Robust as a pattern]**
- **Effort earns entitlement.** Across about 3,100 consumers, tougher loyalty-program requirements shifted preferences from necessity rewards to luxury rewards. The shift was bigger among guilt-prone people (Kivetz & Simonson 2002a). **[Robust as a pattern]**
- **So does fake excellence, unless money is mentioned.** Kivetz & Zheng (2006) describe two routes to justification: entitlement (effort or excellence) and "it doesn't come out of my income." Their posted manuscript reports:
  - High required effort erased the guilt gap between indulgent and sensible choices.
  - With real choices (139 students), bogus feedback placing participants at the 90th percentile raised the share choosing chocolate over batteries from 46% to 75%.
  - When the task's $5 cash value was mentioned, the effect vanished: 45% vs 45%.

  **[Suggestive for the numbers; consistent pattern]**
- **Licensing.**
  - Imagining a commitment to volunteering made a later luxury choice more likely. The effect weakened when the good deed was attributed to an external motive (Khan & Dhar 2006).
  - Salient past restraint ("I didn't buy it last time") justified a later treat (Mukhopadhyay & Johar 2009).
  - Caveat: the wider moral-licensing literature shows a meta-analytic d = 0.31 (Blanken et al. 2015), but bias-corrected estimates range from −0.05 to 0.18 (Kuper & Bott 2019).

  **[Suggestive; contested]**
- **Many people under-indulge, and know it.**
  - A sizeable minority chose luxury prizes over cash of equal or greater value, precisely to force themselves to indulge (Kivetz & Simonson 2002b).
  - With time, people regret their virtue more than their vice, because guilt fades and "missing out" doesn't (Kivetz & Keinan 2006).

  **[Suggestive]**
- **A functional alibi helps.** Adding a small practical feature to an indulgent product is reported to reduce guilt (Keinan, Kivetz & Netzer 2016; I only accessed a secondary summary, not the primary abstract). **[Suggestive]**
- **Reasons change forecasts and memories, not feelings.** People predict and later "remember" feeling worse after indulging without a reason. Reports taken in the moment, and about their last indulgence, show no difference (Xu & Schwarz 2009). **[Suggestive]**

**Can a humorous external justification lower the guilt barrier?** No study tests this directly. The pieces:

- guilt bites at the moment of decision (Okada; Xu & Schwarz);
- entitlement framing works even when the evidence is bogus (Kivetz & Zheng);
- humour blunts resistance to persuasion, mainly by distracting people (Strick et al. 2012).

So an absurd, official-sounding justification should lower *anticipated* guilt at the click. Because it is so obviously external and silly, it should license real-world spending less strongly (Khan & Dhar), and that is exactly what you want. **[Inference]**

## 3. Mental accounting and folk justification

**The science:**

- **Money isn't fungible in people's heads.** We keep mental accounts and enjoy "transaction utility", the thrill of the deal apart from the item. We also frame outcomes to feel better, splitting gains apart and lumping losses together (Thaler 1985, 1999). **[Robust]**
- **Paying hurts in the moment.**
  - Consumption already paid for is enjoyed "as if free", so people prefer to prepay, and cards loosen the link between buying and paying.
  - The authors name the tension: for *pleasure*, you want consumption decoupled from payment; for *good decisions*, you want people aware of the cost (Prelec & Loewenstein 1998).

  **[Robust]**
- **Brain scans predict purchases, modestly.** In fMRI (N = 26), liking a product activated the nucleus accumbens, and excessive prices activated the insula. Brain activity predicted purchases with about 60% accuracy against a 50% chance level: a real signal, but a modest one (Knutson et al. 2007). **[Suggestive]**
- **Tightwads and spendthrifts.** People differ in how much paying hurts. "Tightwads" feel too much pain and underspend; "spendthrifts" feel too little and overspend (Rick, Cryder & Loewenstein 2008). They tend to marry each other, and the mismatch predicts conflict over money and lower marital well-being (Rick, Small & Finkel 2011). Gary's testimonial is peer-reviewed. **[Suggestive]**
- **Flexible accounting.**
  - An expense that could belong to more than one mental account is more likely to be incurred (Cheema & Soman 2006).
  - Framing a cost as "pennies a day" makes it feel trivial (Gourville 1998).
  - The felt cost of a past payment fades with time (Gourville & Soman 1998).

  **[Robust as a set]**
- **Zero is special.** A zero price doesn't just lower the cost; it makes the item feel better (Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely 2007). **[Robust]**

**The culture** (evidence of how people talk, not why they buy):

- **"Girl math" (2023).** It started on a New Zealand radio show on 30 July 2023 and went viral on TikTok from 2 August.
  - Typical rules: under $5 is basically free, cash and gift cards don't count, cost per wear, and a refund is profit.
  - Fortune reported the hashtag at 45M views by mid-August. Critics called it infantilising, and "boy math" parodies followed within weeks.
  - Every one of those rules has an academic twin (Cheema & Soman, Gourville, Thaler). **[Lore/cultural]**
- **"Treat Yo' Self"** comes from *Parks and Recreation*, season 4, episode 4 (13 Oct 2011). **[Lore/cultural]**
- **"Doom spending" (2024).** In a Credit Karma/Qualtrics survey of 1,001 US adults (25–29 Oct 2024):
  - 27% said they doom spend (Gen Z 37%, millennials 39%);
  - 47% of Gen Z said they spend to cope with emotions;
  - 19% of respondents had no savings.

  This is a vendor survey. **[Lore/cultural]**
- **"Little treat" culture and the lipstick effect.**
  - The one rigorous test found that recession cues *reduced* desire for most products but *increased* women's desire for beauty products, through mate-attraction motives (Hill et al. 2012).
  - The general "small treats in hard times" story is plausible but unproven.
  - I could not verify a reputable primary source on "little treat culture" itself in this pass. **[Lore/cultural]**

## 4. Anticipation: the joy of "it's on its way"

- **Savouring has a price.** Students would pay more for a kiss from their favourite celebrity delivered in three days than in three hours (Loewenstein 1987, as summarised by Dunn, Gilbert & Wilson 2011). **[Robust classic]**
- **Looking forward beats looking back.** Across five experiments, people felt more intense emotions anticipating events than remembering them (Van Boven & Ashworth 2007). **[Robust]**
- **Waiting for experiences beats waiting for stuff.** Waiting for experiences is more pleasurable and exciting than waiting for possessions. The evidence spans four studies, including a large experience-sampling study and news reports of people in queues (Kumar, Killingsworth & Gilovich 2014). **[Suggestive to robust]**
- **The anticipation bump is real but brief.** Among 1,530 Dutch adults, the 974 vacationers were happier than non-vacationers *before* their trip. Afterwards there was no difference, except after "very relaxed" trips, and even that boost was significant for only about two weeks (Nawijn et al. 2010). **[Suggestive]**
- **Wanting beats having, for some.** Highly materialistic consumers feel a high before buying and a decline after. Wanting may be more pleasurable than owning (Richins 2013). **[Suggestive]**
- **Design rules from a review** (Dunn, Gilbert & Wilson 2011):
  - pay now, consume later;
  - buy many small pleasures rather than a few big ones, because happiness tracks how *often* you feel good more than how intensely;
  - novelty, surprise and uncertainty slow down how fast you get used to things.

  **[Review; underlying evidence of mixed strength]**
- **Showing the work makes waits feel worth it.** People preferred websites that *visibly worked* through a longer wait over ones that returned identical results instantly: the "labor illusion" (Buell & Norton 2011). Telling people how long the wait will be improves reactions to moderate waits (Hui & Tse 1996). **[Suggestive]**
- The idea that package tracking gives a "dopamine hit" is **[Lore]**. I found no direct study.

## 5. Surprise and uncertain rewards (for any mystery order)

**The brain:**

- **Dopamine signals surprise.** Dopamine neurons encode *prediction errors*: an unexpected reward produces a burst, a predicted one little response, and an omitted one a dip (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997). **[Robust]**
- **Surprise isn't liking.** In 25 adults, unpredictable squirts of juice and water drove more activity in reward regions than predictable ones. Stated preferences did not track that activity (Berns et al. 2001). **[Suggestive]**

**The pleasure of not knowing:**

- **Uncertainty prolongs good moods.** A small unexplained gift (a dollar coin on a puzzling card) kept people's good mood going longer when they were uncertain about it. Forecasters overwhelmingly predicted they'd prefer certainty (Wilson et al. 2005). **[Suggestive: three settings]**
- **One mystery gift can beat two known ones.** People told they would get *a* gift, but not which, stayed happier longer. Uncertain participants who got one gift ended happier than certain participants who got two (Kurtz, Wilson & Gilbert 2007). **[Suggestive]**
- **It works when people can picture the prizes.** A "sure-win" draw with an unknown prize gave greater and longer pleasure, as long as people could vividly imagine the attractive prizes (Lee & Qiu 2009). **[Suggestive]**
- **Teasing helps.** Creating and then resolving a small uncertainty improved experiences and attitudes to ads, yet people don't choose it for themselves (Ruan, Hsee & Lu 2018). **[Suggestive: seven studies]**
- **Uncertain rewards can motivate more than certain ones.** 70% of people offered "$1 or $2" finished drinking 1.4 L of water in 2 minutes, against 43% of those offered a sure $2 (N = 87). This only held when people focused on the process, not the outcome (Shen, Fishbach & Hsee 2015). **[Suggestive]**
- **Optimism fills the gap, unless people do the maths.** An uncertain promotion ("a soda or a less-desirable popcorn") was as appealing as a sure soda. When people deliberately weighed the odds, certainty won (Goldsmith & Amir 2010). **[Suggestive]**
- **Emotional decisions like surprise; analytical ones don't.** Uncertainty about a free gift raised purchases when the decision was emotional and lowered them when it was analytical (Laran & Tsiros 2013). **[Suggestive]**

**Managing disappointment:**

- **Surprises hit harder both ways.**
  - Unexpected outcomes carry more emotional weight, and any outcome feels worse when a better one was possible (Mellers et al. 1997).
  - Unexpected bad outcomes feel worse than expected ones (Shepperd & McNulty 2002).
  - People deliberately lower their expectations to avoid disappointment (van Dijk, Zeelenberg & van der Pligt 2003).

  **[Robust]**
- **Low expectations help.** Sloppily wrapped gifts from friends were liked *more* once opened, because the wrapping lowered expectations (Rixom, Mas & Rixom 2020). **[Suggestive]**
- **Comparisons hurt material purchases.** People are less satisfied with material purchases because they compare them with what they didn't pick or what others got (Carter & Gilovich 2010). **[Suggestive]**
- **Surprise pleasure is cultural.** East Asian participants reported less surprise and less pleasure from unexpected gifts, except when the gift was framed as luck (Valenzuela, Mellers & Strebel 2010). **[Suggestive]**

**Where surprise tips into compulsion:**

- **Surprises and curiosity drive behaviour.** Surprise coupons increase basket size and unplanned buying (Heilman, Nakamoto & Rao 2002). Curiosity can push people to resolve uncertainty even when they expect a bad outcome, electric shocks included (Hsee & Ruan 2016).
- **Loot boxes.** Spending on them correlates with problem-gambling severity:
  - η² = .054 across 7,422 gamers, with problem gamblers spending about $20–40 a month vs $1–5 for others (Zendle & Cairns 2018);
  - η² = .051 in a replication with 1,172 gamers (Zendle & Cairns 2019);
  - positive in 12 of 13 publications, mean r = .27 (Spicer et al. 2022).

  Causality is unclear. Physical card booster packs showed no link, or only a trivial one, in 726 players (Zendle et al. 2021).
- **Blind boxes.**
  - They are argued to be gambling-like physical products. Shanghai's 2022 compliance guidelines recommend age limits, published odds and "pity" guarantees (Xiao 2022, preprint).
  - Blind-box consumer research is mostly cross-sectional surveys. A typical finding: uncertainty raises purchase intention through "emotional value" (Zhang & Zhang 2022). **[Suggestive/weak]**
- **Takeaway:** the pleasant ingredient (uncertainty about *which* good thing you get) can be separated from the compulsive ones: prizes of different value, rarity tiers, fast repeat buying and resale value.

## 6. After the click: post-decision psychology

- **Choosing makes you like what you chose.**
  - After a choice, the chosen item rises in people's estimation and the rejected one falls (Brehm 1956).
  - Chen & Risen (2010) showed that much of this could be a measurement artifact.
  - A meta-analysis of artifact-free studies still finds d = 0.40 [0.32, 0.49] across 43 studies and 2,191 people (Enisman, Shpitzer & Kleiman 2021).

  **[Robust]**
- **Irreversible feels better.** Photography students who could swap their chosen print liked it less, yet most people want the option to change (Gilbert & Ebert 2002). **[Suggestive: one paper]**
- **Regret is its own thing.** It is distinct from dissatisfaction, driven by counterfactual thinking, affected by how reversible the decision is, and it lowers repurchase (Tsiros & Mittal 2000). **[Suggestive: one multi-study paper]**
- **Closure and ritual help.**
  - Physical closure acts, like covering or "turning the page" on rejected options, raised satisfaction after difficult choices (Gu, Botti & Faro 2013).
  - Sealing a regret in an envelope reduced its sting (Li, Wei & Soman 2010).
  - Performing a small ritual made chocolate, lemonade and even carrots more enjoyable, and it had to be *your own* ritual (Vohs et al. 2013).

  **[Suggestive: no large replications found]**
- **Sharing adds to it.** Telling others about good events raises positive feelings beyond the event itself (Gable et al. 2004). **[Robust-ish]**
- **Black Friday is already a ritual.** In 38 interviews with experienced female Black Friday shoppers, the themes were family bonding, strategic planning, "the great race" and *mission accomplished* (Thomas & Peters 2011). **[Qualitative]**
- **Choice overload is conditional.**
  - In the famous jam study, 24 jams drew more shoppers than 6 (60% vs 40% stopped), but 3% bought vs 30% (Iyengar & Lepper 2000).
  - A meta-analysis of 50 experiments (5,036 people) found a mean effect of about zero, D = 0.02 (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd 2010).
  - A later meta-analysis (99 observations, 7,202 people) found overload appears when the option set is complex, the task is hard, preferences are unclear or people want to minimise effort (Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman 2015).

  **[Contested / conditional]**
- **Maximizers suffer.** People who always seek the best option report less happiness and more regret, and are less satisfied with their purchases (Schwartz et al. 2002). **[Suggestive: largely correlational]**
- **"Decision fatigue" didn't replicate.** The underlying effect is ego depletion.
  - A 23-lab registered replication (2,141 people) found d = 0.04, with a confidence interval that includes zero (Hagger et al. 2016).
  - A 36-lab test (3,531 people) found d = 0.06, with the data favouring "no effect" about 4 to 1 (Vohs et al. 2021).

  **[Not robust]** Use it as a joke only.

## 7. Ethics and harm

- **How common is compulsive buying?**
  - The pooled prevalence is 4.9% in representative adult samples, 8.3% among students, 12.3% in non-representative adult samples and 16.2% in shopping-specific samples.
  - Being younger and female are associated with it (Maraz, Griffiths & Demetrovics 2016: 40 studies, about 32,000 people). **[Robust, with large heterogeneity]**
  - Clinicians describe urges that feel irresistible, guilt and regret after buying, and money problems (Müller et al. 2019). For many, relief comes only once a purchase is made (as cited in Maraz et al. 2016).
- **Who is vulnerable?**
  - Sad people pay more (Garg et al. 2018).
  - People with mental-health problems are about 3.5 times as likely to be in problem debt (18% vs 5%), and impulsivity is a common symptom (Money and Mental Health Policy Institute).
  - Buy-now-pay-later (BNPL) users are more likely to be highly indebted, to carry credit-card balances and to be behind on payments (CFPB 2023).
  - 47% of Gen Z say they spend to cope with emotions (Credit Karma 2024).
- **Your own levers are the risky ones:**
  - humour lowers resistance (Strick et al. 2012);
  - uncertainty increases what people invest (Shen et al. 2015);
  - surprises trigger unplanned buying (Heilman et al. 2002);
  - "you deserve it" works even when the evidence is fake (Kivetz & Zheng 2006).
- **The prosocial alternative.** Spending on others raised happiness in the original study (Dunn, Aknin & Norton 2008). A large registered replication gave mixed results (Aknin et al. 2020):
  - confirmed with a real purchase (712 people);
  - no effect in one recall study (1,950 people);
  - a small effect in a larger recall study (5,199 people).

  **[Robust but small]**
- **The cultural counterweight.** Adbusters' *Buy Nothing Day* (since 1992) has been held on Black Friday since 1997. A site that gives you the relief of ordering while you buy nothing sits neatly between the two. **[Lore/cultural]**

## What this means for Order That Shit

1. **Make the free order the hero, and say so.**
   - Choosing is the medicine, not paying (Rick et al. 2014, Exp. 1), and "free" is disproportionately attractive (Shampanier et al. 2007).
   - Copy: *"No money leaves your account. The feeling does."* and *"You don't need to buy anything to feel this."*
2. **Build a Permission Engine: the "Official Justification™".** After the Shit Finder, issue a stamped, official-sounding reason. Use three routes plus an alibi:
   - effort: *"You have survived 14 meetings that could have been emails"*;
   - bogus excellence: *"Our AI ranks your suffering in the top 3% nationally"*;
   - restraint: *"You have not ordered anything in 9 days"*;
   - functional alibi: *"Technically, it's for work."*

   **Never** use money framing ("you can afford it", "you've earned €50"): mentioning money wiped out the entitlement effect (Kivetz & Zheng 2006). Show the justification *before* the Order button, because that's where guilt lives (Okada 2005; Xu & Schwarz 2009).
3. **Let people choose, then honour the choice.**
   - Two or three quick taps (category, urgency, "how much shit?") restore control; a verdict alone doesn't.
   - Never "override" a pick in the fake flow (Rick et al. 2014, Exp. 2).
   - Keep the options few (Chernev et al. 2015).
4. **Add a before/after "Shit-o-meter".**
   - A 0–100 mood slider before and after ordering mirrors how the retail-therapy study measured sadness.
   - With anonymous, opt-in data, "mood before vs after ordering" becomes a *true* chart for The Science™.
   - Joke the boundary: *"Angry? That's a different department."* Choosing doesn't fix anger.
5. **Bless the long-considered purchase; slow down the impulse.** Ask *"How long has it been in your cart?"*
   - More than 7 days: full blessing and certificate. That fits the owner's intent and the research on people under-indulging (Kivetz & Keinan 2006; Kivetz & Simonson 2002b).
   - Less than 24 hours: *"The Science™ requires a 24-hour marinade. Your certificate will be waiting."*
   - That's a cooling-off guardrail disguised as a bit, and the wait adds anticipation anyway.
6. **Make the Certificate a closure ritual.**
   - Stress finality: *"This order is irreversible"* (Gilbert & Ebert 2002).
   - Add a tap-to-stamp or seal-the-envelope animation that the user performs (Vohs et al. 2013; Li et al. 2010; Gu et al. 2013).
   - Certify the *decision*, not the pain: "Certified Decisive Human", never "Certified Sad Person". Symbolic purchases that remind people of their shortcoming backfire; validation helps (Lisjak et al. 2015; Mandel et al. 2017).
   - Make it shareable in one tap (Gable et al. 2004).
7. **Turn "it's on its way" into theatre.**
   - Build a fake tracking page that *shows the work*: "Shit being hand-selected by Gary", "Shit clearing customs (emotionally)" (Buell & Norton 2011).
   - Frame the wait as an experience: "your shit is on a gap year" (Kumar et al. 2014).
   - Keep the *timing* certain ("arrives in 3–5 business moods") and put the uncertainty in the contents, not the ETA (Hui & Tse 1996; Wilson et al. 2005).
8. **Spread the joy across small moments.** Confirmation, then "dispatched", then "out for delivery", then the certificate reveal beats one big confetti burst (Thaler 1985; Dunn et al. 2011).
9. **Design the mystery reveal as "guaranteed good, uncertain which".** This applies to whatever format survives brief 06: a €1 order, a certificate with a random sticker, or a free "Shit Roulette".
   - Show the *whole pool* as a gallery so people can imagine the possibilities (Lee & Qiu 2009). This also covers disclosure.
   - Tease, then reveal after a short beat (Ruan et al. 2018; Kurtz et al. 2007).
   - No rarity tiers, "legendary" items or "you won" language. A better outcome that you didn't get turns the ordinary result into a loss (Mellers et al. 1997).
   - Lower expectations on purpose: *"Expectations: low. Shit: guaranteed."* (Shepperd & McNulty 2002; van Dijk et al. 2003).
10. **"You pick the vibe, fate picks the shit."** Let users pick the pool theme (Desk Shit, Kitchen Shit, Emotional Support Shit). That keeps a sense of control inside the mystery. Frame it emotionally, not as odds to calculate (Laran & Tsiros 2013; Goldsmith & Amir 2010).
11. **End the reveal with a stop cue, not a re-buy button.**
    - *"Go enjoy your shit."*
    - On anything that costs money: no "open another", no streaks, no bundles, no countdowns. Uncertainty drives people to invest more, and curiosity pulls them back (Shen et al. 2015; Hsee & Ruan 2016).
    - Let the free joke order carry the viral load.
12. **Make the guardrail part of the joke: sometimes the Shit Finder prescribes nothing.**
    - When the input mentions debt, being broke, overdraft or "can't stop buying", answer: *"Diagnosis: you have ordered enough shit. Prescription: this certificate."*
    - Show a support link, and no paid call to action (Maraz et al. 2016; Money and Mental Health Policy Institute; Rick et al. 2008).
13. **Add a gift mode: "Order that shit for someone else".** Send a friend a certificate and a justification. Spending on others boosts happiness modestly (Dunn et al. 2008; Aknin et al. 2020), and it doubles as a share loop.
14. **Position it for Black Friday: "The only Black Friday deal where you save 100%."**
    - The site is Black Friday and Buy Nothing Day at once.
    - The certificate is the "mission accomplished" moment of the Black Friday ritual (Thomas & Peters 2011).
    - It speaks to doom spenders without taking their money (Credit Karma 2024).
15. **Mine real science for The Science™.**
    - Real findings are funnier than invented ones: *"Brain scans predict purchases 60% of the time. A coin: 50%."* (Knutson et al. 2007).
    - Present zombie findings like decision fatigue or the jam study only as obvious satire, never as advice.

## Ethical guardrails

1. **The feel-good core stays free**, and the page says so.
2. **Keep the jokes and the money apart.** Fake tickers, never-ending sales and countdowns live only in the joke flow. Any page that takes real money is plain, visibly separate, and free of scarcity or urgency cues (see brief 06 for EU unfair-practice rules).
3. **Rules for any paid mystery:**
   - every item in the pool has equal value and is shown;
   - no rarity tiers and no "win" language;
   - one flat price, with a per-person cap (for example 1 a day and a small campaign total);
   - no bundles, subscriptions, auto-reorder or BNPL;
   - buyers must be 18+.
4. **Route distress away from payment.** If the input mentions money stress or distress, remove every paid call to action and show "order nothing" plus a support link.
5. **Keep mood data anonymous.** Never tie mood or sadness inputs to an identity, and never retarget ads on them.
6. **Use stop cues, not loops.** No streaks, no "open another", no re-engagement nudges tied to a purchase.
7. **Keep science claims honest** outside the obviously satirical charts.
8. **Label fiction as fiction** (testimonials, tickers), and never imitate a real retailer's branding.
