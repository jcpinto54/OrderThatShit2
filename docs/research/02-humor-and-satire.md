# 02 — Humor and satire: what makes things funny, what makes satire land, and why people share it

*Compiled 2026-09-29 by a research agent; verify before quoting publicly.*

**Evidence tags.** **[Robust]** means a meta-analysis, several independent studies or large field data. **[Suggestive]** means one or a few studies, small or narrow samples, correlational data, or a contested result. **[Lore]** means comedy-practitioner craft with no direct experimental test.

**Method.** Every citation was checked against DOI metadata (Crossref), PubMed, a publisher page or an author PDF. The web-search quota ran out partway through, so a few findings rest on an abstract or a secondary summary. Those are flagged, and the section "What I could not verify" lists them.

## TL;DR

- **Funny means a violation that feels OK at the same moment.** Benign Violation Theory (BVT) is the best-supported model: something seems wrong and safe at once. A review of 20+ theories found that violation, benign appraisal and simultaneity separate funny from unfunny; surprise and superiority don't. Most evidence comes from one lab, and critics call it stretchy. **[Suggestive→Robust]**
- **Distance is the dial.** Mild mishaps are funnier up close. Tragedies need distance (time, fiction, strangers), but too much distance kills the joke. **[Suggestive]**
- **Humor makes ads liked, not believed.** Across 369 correlations, humor raised attention and ad liking (brand attitude about half as much) but lowered source credibility. The negative feelings an ad triggers predict brand damage better than funniness predicts gain. **[Robust]**
- **Amusement spreads; outrage spreads without selling.** Views driven by humor keep their persuasiveness and views driven by outrage lose it. It works best when the brand *is* the joke and lands last. **[Robust-ish]**
- **Satire is read through the viewer's lens, and a minority takes it literally.** An in-voice "we are a humor site" label cut belief and sharing better than fact-checks did. **[Suggestive]**
- **Profanity works in small doses.** One swear word makes a review more helpful and amplifies what it describes. The effect vanishes when swearing piles up and weakens when censored. "Swearers are honest" is contested. Google Ads bans profanity in ads *and destinations*. **[Suggestive→Robust]**
- **Feel-better humor is kind.** Good-natured humor and humorous reappraisal soothe more than mean humor or serious reappraisal. Self-enhancing and affiliative styles go with better mental health, and self-defeating with worse. People in a bad mood enjoy dark humor *less*. **[Robust direction; small effects]**
- **For Order That Shit (OTS):** the game is *one undefined product, sold with total corporate conviction as the cure for everything*. Heighten the problems' specificity and the corporation's commitment. Keep the customer as the hero and marketing as the butt, and end on a small, warm silver lining. **Add a tragedy guard to the Shit Finder now:** any input containing "help" gets "Help is not available at this time."

## 1. What makes things funny

| Theory | Core claim | What the evidence says |
|---|---|---|
| **Superiority** (Plato, Hobbes) | We laugh at others' inferiority | It explains who laughs and who gets hurt; social identity theory looks most promising for disparagement humor (Ferguson & Ford 2008). It does *not* separate funny from unfunny (Warren, Barsky & McGraw 2021). |
| **Relief** (Spencer, Freud) | Laughter discharges tension | "Tension, then safety" is a real trigger, and BVT's "benign" half captures it. Claims about health and stress-buffering are weak (Martin 2001). |
| **Incongruity-resolution** (Suls 1972) | An expectation is broken, then made sense of by a hidden rule | This is the base of most modern theories. But incongruity has been defined at least four ways (surprise, juxtaposition, atypicality, violation), and on its own it does not separate funny from amazing, tragic or puzzling (Warren & McGraw 2016a). Resolvable incongruity and pure nonsense are distinct humor families (Ruch, McGhee & Hehl 1990). |
| **Benign Violation** (McGraw & Warren 2010; Warren & McGraw 2016a) | Humor happens when a violation is simultaneously appraised as benign | In 5 studies, benign moral violations produced laughter along with disgust. In 6 studies using entertainment, products and social interactions, BVT separated humorous from non-humorous experiences better than incongruity did. It builds on Veatch (1998). |
| **Inside Jokes** (Hurley, Dennett & Adams 2011) | Mirth rewards catching a wrong assumption the mind had quietly committed to | Elegant, but has had little direct testing (my assessment). As a writing tool: plant a confident assumption, then expose it. |
| **Play signal** (Gervais & Wilson 2005) | Laughter evolved as contagious play that signalled safe times | An evolutionary synthesis: "benign" is a play frame. |

**Ways to make a violation benign** (McGraw & Warren 2010): an alternative norm that says it is okay, weak commitment to the violated norm, or psychological distance.

**Distance × severity.** Across five studies, tragedies were funnier when distant in time, socially, hypothetically or spatially, and mild mishaps were funnier when close (McGraw, Warren, Williams & Leonard 2012). Jokes about Hurricane Sandy rose in funniness, peaked and fell over 100 days, which the authors call a "comedic sweet spot" (McGraw, Williams & Warren 2014). **[Suggestive]**

**The integrative review** found that surprise is not required (Warren, Barsky & McGraw 2021). A running catchphrase can stay funny as long as each new violation feels fresh.

**Critiques and limits.** BVT's own coding scheme counts as violations everything from threats and insults to sarcasm, bad manners and anything illogical (Warren & McGraw 2016b). That is broad enough to fit almost any joke after the fact (my assessment). Oring (2016) argues that humor is cognitive ("appropriate incongruity") rather than an emotional appraisal. Westbury & Hollis (2019) call humor theories post hoc descriptions that cannot predict *how* funny something will be. The evidence also leans on one lab, self-reports and mostly US samples. Use BVT as a checklist, not a law.

**Laughter is social.** People are overwhelmingly more likely to laugh with others, and hearing laughter makes weak jokes funnier (Cai et al. 2019). Social laughter released endogenous opioids in a small PET study (N = 12), which fits a bonding function (Manninen et al. 2017). **[Suggestive]**

## 2. Humor in advertising and consumer behavior

- **The meta-analysis** (Eisend 2009; 369 correlations). Humor improved attention, positive affect and attitude toward the ad. It also improved brand attitude and purchase intent, but the ad-attitude effect was about twice the brand-attitude effect. It lowered source credibility and had no reliable effect on cognitions. The mechanism looks affective. Eisend (2011), as summarized by Warren, Barsky & McGraw (2018), points to mood enhancement as a key persuasion route. **[Robust]**
- **No guarantee.** Weinberger & Gulas (1992) concluded that humor "is by no means a guarantee of better ads"; results depend on audience, situation and type of humor (their 2019 update reviews 250+ works). Humor suits low-risk, hedonic products (summarized in Warren & McGraw 2016b). It helps brands people already like and *backfires* when prior attitudes are negative (Chattopadhyay & Basu 1990). **[Robust]**
- **Being funny is not enough.** Across 3 experiments and 1 correlational study, humorous marketing hurt brands when its violation was *highly threatening*, mocked *a specific group* rather than people in general, or triggered *avoidance*, such as disgust around food (Warren & McGraw 2016b). In one comparison, two equally funny ads produced brand attitudes of 4.53 and 3.10. Logic violations (their example: an airplane with legs) invite approach and are the safest kind. Across four samples of ads, brand attitudes tracked the negative emotions an ad triggered more than how funny it was (Warren, Carter & McGraw 2019). **[Robust-ish]**
- **Humor disarms resistance.** Pairing a product with humor raised implicit liking and choice, even though the product was recognized *less* (Strick et al. 2009, *JEP: Applied*). Humor also blocks negative brand associations (Strick et al. 2012). **[Suggestive: lab studies]**
- **Humor shrinks problems.** After a humorous PSA, people judged the issue less important and searched less for help (McGraw, Schiro & Fernbach 2015). **[Suggestive]**
- **Failed humor costs status.** In 8 experiments, successful jokes raised status and inappropriate ones lowered it (Bitterly, Brooks & Schweitzer 2017).
- **Wear-out.** "Humorous ads wear out faster" is **[Lore]**: the main tests are small 1980s radio studies I could not access (Gelb & Zinkhan 1985, 1986). Better supported: for low-involvement audiences, varying *surface* features across repetitions beats varying substance (Schumann, Petty & Clemons 1990).

## 3. Humor and sharing

- **Emotion and arousal.** In three months of *New York Times* articles, positive content was more viral than negative. High-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) spread more, and sadness less (Berger & Milkman 2012). **[Robust]** Caveat: Berger's (2011) small lab study (N = 40), in which physiological arousal increased sharing, **failed two replications** (N = 111 and N = 160; Prowten et al. 2024). "Emotional content spreads" holds up better than "arousal causes sharing".
- **Amusement specifically.** Two field studies of video-ad sharing across platforms coded 11 emotions and more than 60 ad traits (Tellis et al. 2019). Amusement, excitement, inspiration and warmth increased sharing. Information-heavy content and *early or persistent brand placement* reduced it, and moderate length (1.2–1.7 min) did best. In about 800 videos, high arousal was the main driver of sharing (Nelson-Field, Riebe & Newstead 2013). **[Robust-ish]**
- **Views versus value.** Across 400 video ads and 24,000 survey responses, persuasiveness was on average about 10% lower for every million views. The exceptions were ads whose views came from humor rather than outrageousness, and ads whose comments named the product (Tucker 2015). Emotional ads get shared and informative ads help the brand; emotional ads in which *the brand is integral* do both (Akpinar & Berger 2017). **[Robust-ish]**
- **Controversy follows an inverted U.** Moderate controversy increases conversation; high controversy suppresses it through discomfort (Chen & Berger 2013). **[Suggestive]**
- **Meme styles.** In 1,000 memes from one Facebook page, *self-defeating* humor was the most viral style, and humor types (sarcasm, pun, exaggeration and so on) did not differ (Taecharungroj & Nueangjamnong 2015). **[Suggestive: one page]**
- **Humorous complaining.** Across six studies, humorous complaints helped people warn, entertain and make a good impression, though they got less redress (McGraw, Warren & Kan 2015). They were shared more on Facebook and rated more helpful on Amazon (per Warren et al. 2018). **The Shit Finder is a humorous-complaint generator.**
- **Why people send a joke to one particular person.** **[Suggestive; direct evidence is thin]** Word of mouth serves impression management and social bonding (Berger 2014). Encryption theory holds that jokes encode shared knowledge, so "getting it" signals "we're alike" (Flamson & Barrett 2008; formalized by Smaldino, Flamson & McElreath 2018). People who recognized themselves in depression memes rated them more relatable *and shareable* (Akram et al. 2020).

## 4. Satire, irony and "Is this real?"

- **Viewers project themselves onto the satirist.** Conservatives and liberals found Stephen Colbert equally funny, but conservatives more often believed he only pretended to joke and meant what he said (LaMarre, Landreville & Beam 2009; N = 332). Conservatives also appreciated irony and exaggeration less, partly through lower need for cognition (Young et al. 2019; N = 305). Young (2020) frames satire's appeal as ambiguity and play, against outrage's certainty. **[Suggestive]**
- **Two routes that pull in opposite directions.** Absorption in satire reduced counterarguing, so the critique landed; *finding it funny* made attitudes toward the satirized subject more positive (Boukes et al. 2015). Jokes can also lower argument scrutiny (Young 2008) while making the issue seem less serious (Nabi, Moyer-Gusé & Byrne 2007, per Warren et al. 2018).
- **Gentle versus bitter satire.** Horatian (playful) and Juvenalian (contemptuous) satire had different effects on perceived humor and counterarguing, depending on the audience (Holbert et al. 2011). OTS is Horatian.
- **Poe's law.** Without a clear marker of intent, parody can be indistinguishable from the real thing (Aikin 2013). In a six-month survey of more than 800 Americans, 8 of 23 Babylon Bee satire items were confidently believed by at least 15% of Republicans, and nearly 1 in 8 Democrats believed an Onion-invented quote (Garrett, Bond & Poulsen 2019; a summary in *The Conversation*, not peer-reviewed). Satire seemed most "real" to heavy satire viewers who watched little hard news (Balmas 2014). Senders overestimate how well sarcasm comes across in email (Kruger et al. 2005).
- **In-voice labels work.** In two experiments (N = 218 and N = 610), a flag saying the publisher self-identifies as humor reduced belief and sharing, while fact-checker and peer flags showed no benefit. The humor flag also produced less resentment (Garrett & Poulsen 2019). **[Suggestive]**
- **The upside.** In more than 2.5 million comments, people often met misinformation with open disbelief (Metzger et al. 2021). "Is this real?" threads are engagement.
- **Satirizing consumerism and advertising:**
  - *Ironic consumption*: people read irony when a product clashes with the user's identity, and they judge ironic users less kindly when the product belongs to *their own* group (Warren & Mohr 2019). Keep the irony legible, and don't sneer at the fans.
  - *The charm of three*: impressions peak at three positive claims, and a fourth makes people skeptical of all of them (Shu & Carlson 2014). Parody ads blow through that ceiling on purpose, and that is the joke.
  - *Charts don't reliably persuade*: the finding that trivial graphs boost belief (Tal & Wansink 2016) failed four replications, N = 623 (Dragicevic & Jansen 2018). Cornell later found Wansink guilty of misconduct in other work. The Science™ works because the trope is recognizable.

## 5. Profanity and taboo

- **Swearing in word of mouth** (Lafreniere, Moore & Fisher 2022; 2 field datasets and 4 experiments). Reviews containing a swear word were rated more helpful. Swearing amplified attitudes toward the attribute it qualified: "damn quiet" helped the product, "damn loud" hurt it. **The effect vanished when there were many swear words, and it was stronger uncensored or euphemistic ("darn") than censored ("d*mn").** **[Robust-ish]**
- **Honesty is contested.** Feldman et al. (2017) linked profanity to honesty in a lab study (N = 276), 73,789 Facebook users and 50 US states. De Vries et al. (2018) argued that the lab inference was flawed, and in two other datasets profanity predicted *lower* other-rated honesty-humility and *more* actual cheating. Don't bank on "swearing = authentic".
- **Impressions.** Speakers who swore in written conversations were rated lower on overall impression, intelligence and trustworthiness (DeFrank & Kahlbaugh 2019; N = 138). **[Suggestive]**
- **Relief.** Repeating a swear word raised pain tolerance by 33% compared with a neutral word (Stephens & Robertson 2020, N = 92, replicating Stephens, Atkins & Kingston 2009).
- **BVT at the level of single words.** Across 4,997 words rated by 821 people, the funniest were mild bodily or sexual taboo words and silly insults ("booty", "ass", "nitwit"), and the least funny were violent or traumatic ones ("rape", "torture", "death") (Engelthaler & Hills 2018). Mild taboo reads as a benign violation.
- **Disgust.** Amusement and disgust can co-occur (Hemenover & Schimmack 2007), but disgust drives avoidance (Warren & McGraw 2016b). The *word* "shit" is a mild linguistic violation; poop *imagery* is a purity violation.
- **Second-language speakers.** Polish–English bilinguals chose stronger swear words when translating into English, their second language, though only for slurs aimed at groups (Gawinkowska, Paradowski & Bilewicz 2013). Multilinguals report weaker force for swear words in later-learned languages (Dewaele 2004; details unverified). "Shit" plausibly lands lighter for many non-native readers, but this is untested.
- **Platforms.** Google Ads lists obscene or profane language under "shocking content", and the policy covers ads, assets and destinations. Check each platform's rules (see brief 04).

## 6. Humor as coping: what makes people feel *better*

- **Kind beats mean.** Good-natured humor reduced negative emotion and raised positive emotion more than mean-spirited humor did, in 2 studies that ruled out differences in difficulty and expectations (Samson & Gross 2012).
- **Humorous reappraisal beats serious reappraisal.** Humorous coping worked better in the short term and still helped more with negative emotions a week later (Samson et al. 2014; N = 57; also Kugler & Kuhbandner 2015). Part of the mechanism is distraction: humor soothed more than equally pleasant non-funny material (Strick et al. 2009, *Emotion*). An amusing film sped cardiovascular recovery after a fear film (Fredrickson & Levenson 1998). **[Suggestive: consistent lab results]**
- **Humor styles.** The four styles are affiliative, self-enhancing, aggressive and self-defeating (Martin et al. 2003). A meta-analysis of 37 studies (N = 12,734) found that affiliative and self-enhancing styles relate positively to mental health, self-defeating negatively, and aggressive not at all (Schneider, Voracek & Tran 2018). Warren, Barsky & McGraw (2018) recommend making light of shared misfortunes and general absurdities, not flaws in oneself or in specific others. **[Robust, correlational]**
- **Memes as coping.** In an online experiment (N = 748), memes raised humor and positive emotion, which indirectly raised coping efficacy. Memes captioned *about the stressor* lowered stress (Myrick, Nabi & Eng 2022). People with depressive symptoms found depression memes more humorous, relatable and mood-improving (Akram et al. 2020). **[Suggestive]**
- **The catch with gallows humor.** Emergency workers use black humor to vent, bond and gain distance (Rowe & Regehr 2010). Yet enjoyment of black humor was highest among people with no mood disturbance and lowest among the most disturbed (Willinger et al. 2017; N = 156). **The people you most want to comfort enjoy dark jokes least.**
- **Caveats.** Humor makes problems feel smaller (McGraw, Schiro & Fernbach 2015), which is good for small problems and bad for serious ones. Health claims are weak (Martin 2001).
- **Link to the retail-therapy brief (03).** Making shopping choices, *even hypothetical ones*, reduced lingering sadness by restoring a sense of control (Rick, Pereira & Burson 2014). OTS's fake checkout is a hypothetical choice with a laugh attached.

## 7. Comedy craft: where evidence exists and where it is lore

| Principle | Evidence | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| **Find the game**: spot the first unusual thing, then heighten and explore it ("if this is true, what else is true?") | UCB improv manual (Besser, Roberts & Walsh 2013) | [Lore] |
| **Specificity**: concrete, rare, odd-sounding words | Such words are rated funnier (Westbury & Hollis 2019) | [Suggestive] |
| **Rule of three** | Persuasion analogue: three claims charm, four alarm (Shu & Carlson 2014) | [Lore] |
| **Timing** | In 20 recorded joke performances, tellers didn't pause before punchlines or change speed (Attardo & Pickering 2011); timing is structure | [Suggestive] |
| **Deadpan register clash** | The clash between serious style and absurd content drives humor in "classical art memes" (Piata 2019) | [Suggestive/Lore] |
| **Callbacks and in-jokes** | Encryption theory: reward insiders (Flamson & Barrett 2008) | [Lore+] |
| **Relatability** | Mild mishaps are funnier when close (McGraw et al. 2012); relatable memes are more shareable (Akram et al. 2020) | [Suggestive] |
| **Absurdism for younger audiences** | Nonsense humor's funniness declines with age after the late teens, while resolvable humor rises (Ruch et al. 1990; N = 4,292 Germans). I found no solid research on Gen Z post-irony or anti-humor. | [Suggestive/Lore] |
| **Laughter cues** | Added laughter made bad jokes funnier (Cai et al. 2019); canned laughter from an out-group didn't help (Platow et al. 2005, title and design) | [Suggestive] |
| **Memory** | Humorous sentences are recalled better (Schmidt 1994) but steal memory from nearby content (Warren et al. 2018), so make the brand the joke | [Robust-ish] |
| **AI-written jokes** | In blind ratings, ChatGPT-3.5 matched or beat laypeople and Onion headline writers (Gorenz & Schwarz 2024) | [Suggestive] |

## 8. Cultural differences (brief)

- Humorous ads in Korea, Germany, Thailand and the US share the same incongruity structure, but their content varies with norms such as collectivism (Alden, Hoyer & Lee 1993).
- In Eastern cultures, people view humor less positively and use it less for coping (Jiang, Li & Hou 2019). The link between humor style and mental health also differs between East and West (Schneider et al. 2018).
- How much advertising uses humor varies across 15 countries over 90 years (Karpinska-Krakowiak et al. 2025), and taste varies by nation and class (Kuipers 2015).
- Rule of thumb: visual, situational and deadpan-corporate humor travels well. Puns and US-only references don't. Irony in text gets lost even among native speakers (Kruger et al. 2005).

## What this means for Order That Shit

**1. State the game and heighten only along its three dials.** The game: *a company sells one undefined product and believes, with total corporate sincerity, that it cures every problem.* Underneath the satire sits a warm truth: *it's okay to want one small good thing.* Each new clip or verdict turns one dial:
- the problem gets more specific (but not tragic);
- the corporate apparatus gets bigger (Legal, Science, Customer Success, a Black Friday war room);
- the product gets *more* undefined.

Never define the product; the CEO clip ("I still don't know") is canonical. For Black Friday, ask "if this is true, what else is true?": a 0%-off doorbuster whose savings are "emotional", a returns desk that accepts everything except regret.

**2. The butt is the marketing machine and the company, never the customer or a group.** Exclusive targets hurt brands (Warren & McGraw 2016b). Let the *brand* be the self-deprecating one. That keeps self-defeating humor's shareability (Taecharungroj & Nueangjamnong 2015) without its mental-health cost to viewers (Schneider et al. 2018). Small drift: "Karen" in the ticker is a meme insult.

**3. Ship a tragedy guard in the Shit Finder before the Black Friday push.** Close, mild problems are the sweet spot. Grief, self-harm, illness, abuse and real debt are close *and* severe, and that is not benign (McGraw et al. 2012). Today:
- `help` matches any input containing "help" and replies "Help is not available at this time."
- The generic pool can answer "my dad died" with "Diagnosis: insufficient shit."

The share button then makes it a screenshot. Before keyword matching, catch crisis and grief terms and step out of character: *"The Shit Finder only handles small shit. This sounds like big stuff, and you deserve a real person."* Link to findahelpline.com (verified helplines in 175+ countries), hide sharing, and keep the text out of the share URL. For heavy-but-not-crisis inputs (layoff, divorce), use a gentler verdict where the brand is the joke.

**4. Formalize the four-beat testimonial; the "feel better" lives in beat 4.**
1. A specific small misery.
2. "I ordered that shit."
3. The honesty beat: the problem is unsolved.
4. An absurd silver lining.

Make beat 4 *self-enhancing*: a small real comfort (company, anticipation, a doorbell, a box to lean on), never "I'm pathetic". Linda's "But now it's next to me" is the model. Gary's "The shit had tracking. She did not." is the strongest line, because it is a logic violation about reliability. The season-2 drafts in the working tree already hit the target. Donna ("Nineteen Black Fridays… from bed. Like a professional.") is Black Friday satire with a self-enhancing ending. Walter ("At my age, you don't wait for a sale") is pure permission. Diane is a callback to Gary. One more idea for the corporate dial: *"I'm from Legal. / We were asked to clarify what that shit is. / We cannot. / We were asked to stop saying 'shit'. / We cannot."*

**5. Make the warmth explicit, once per flow.** Kind humor soothes and mean humor doesn't (Samson & Gross 2012), and low moods reject dark jokes (Willinger et al. 2017). End the checkout and the Certificate on one deadpan kindness: *"You did something nice for yourself today. That counts."* One sincere beat may also win back some of the credibility that humor costs (Eisend 2009; this is inference).

**6. Sharpen Shit Finder verdicts: specific, well-matched and sendable.** Substring matching misfires:
- `ex` plus `text` fires on any input containing "text".
- `cat` fires on "vacation", "education" and "communicate".

Use word boundaries. Add verdicts for universal small miseries: the inbox, meetings, the group chat, the Sunday scaries, the 3pm slump, doom-scrolling, "nothing to wear", Black Friday FOMO. Make the broke/debt verdict benign using the site's own truth: *"Good news: that shit is free, because it doesn't exist. Cheapest fix you'll find all Black Friday."* A shared verdict is a humorous complaint (McGraw, Warren & Kan 2015), so point the share copy at a person: *"Send to someone with this exact problem."*

**7. Make the brand the punchline and put it last.** "Order that shit" is always the final beat; no pre-roll logo, because early and persistent branding reduces sharing (Tellis et al. 2019). A joke that *is* the brand is the "emotional and brand-integral" case that gets both sharing and brand benefit (Akpinar & Berger 2017). Stay humor-driven rather than shock-driven (Tucker 2015).

**8. Use "Is this real?" as a hook, and fence it in.**
- Put an in-voice marker on every shareable asset: *"Satire. Nothing is for sale. Nothing ships."* Self-identified humor labels were the most effective and least resented flag (Garrett & Poulsen 2019).
- For AI video, EU AI Act Art. 50(4) allows light-touch disclosure for satire, but some disclosure must exist (see brief 07 §6.1).
- Swap the fake quotes pinned on real organizations (Wired, The Verge, the FDA, TechCrunch) for fictional sources, since they are the items most likely to circulate as real. This is not legal advice.
- Make it impossible to type a real card number into the fake checkout.

**9. Keep profanity to a set dose.** The name already carries the violation.
- Use one "shit" per beat and don't stack them; the benefit vanishes with many swear words (Lafreniere et al. 2022).
- Keep it uncensored on owned channels, since censoring weakens it.
- Never aim it at a person.
- **No poop imagery**: that is a disgust violation, and disgust drives avoidance.
- Because Google's policy covers destinations, paid search is probably off the table. Plan for organic and user-generated reach.

**10. Aim Horatian satire at the Black Friday machine, not at shoppers.** The targets are fake urgency, countdowns, "only 3 left" and AI hype: mild, shared, annual pain. Buy Nothing Day falls on the same Friday in North America and the UK, so OTS can be the one checkout compatible with it ("Order that shit. Buy nothing."). Topical jokes have a sweet spot (McGraw, Williams & Warren 2014), so retire Black Friday bits after Cyber Monday. The parody nags must never really block anyone: real friction is a real violation, not a benign one.

**11. Let user-generated content make the user the hero.** Prompt small wins ("I finally ordered the thing"), not confessions of debt or sadness. Stitch and duet formats add in-group laughter, which lifts perceived funniness (Cai et al. 2019; Platow et al. 2005). The Certificate is an ironic-consumption badge (Warren & Mohr 2019), so make it readable without context and personal, with their item and their problem on it.

**12. Keep the game fixed and vary the surface** (new characters, departments and problems; Schumann, Petty & Clemons 1990). Verdict IDs are already stable, so track share rate per ID and prune the bottom quartile every few weeks.

**13. Use AI for volume and humans for taste.** LLM jokes can match human ones in blind tests (Gorenz & Schwarz 2024). Generate about 20 candidates per slot, have 5–10 target readers rate them blind, and ship the top 10%.

**14. Write for a global audience:** visual, situational and register-clash jokes rather than puns or US-only references. Test verdicts with non-native readers.

### Joke-writing checklist (every clip, every verdict)

1. **Violation:** what is "wrong", in one line?
2. **Benign:** why is it safe? Is it fiction, obviously fake, distant, or the company's fault?
3. **Butt:** is it the marketing machine or the company, and not the viewer or a group?
4. **Specific:** is there one concrete, slightly odd detail?
5. **Game:** does it turn a dial while the product stays undefined?
6. **Punchline last:** does "order that shit" land as the payoff?
7. **Warm beat:** is there a small real comfort or a "you're not alone"?
8. **Screenshot test:** does it still read as satire with no context? If not, add a marker.
9. **Profanity:** at most one swear per beat, never aimed at a person, no poop imagery.
10. **Tragedy test:** would it hurt someone living through this right now? If so, cut it or add distance.
11. **Send test:** name one person you'd send it to.
12. **Blind test:** does it beat the median of the last ten?

## What humor research says NOT to do

- **Punch down** or pick an exclusive target (Warren & McGraw 2016b; Ferguson & Ford 2008).
- **Escalate severity for reach.** Negative reactions outweigh funniness, and outrage-driven views don't persuade (Warren, Carter & McGraw 2019; Tucker 2015).
- **Joke at live tragedies or at crisis inputs** (McGraw et al. 2012, 2014).
- **Make serious problems (debt, illness, loneliness) the core joke.** Humor shrinks how important they seem (McGraw, Schiro & Fernbach 2015).
- **Expect humor to buy credibility** (Eisend 2009).
- **Over-swear, half-censor or use scatological imagery** (Lafreniere et al. 2022; Warren & McGraw 2016b).
- **Assume deadpan irony is obvious,** or put invented words in real organizations' mouths (Kruger et al. 2005; Garrett, Bond & Poulsen 2019).
- **Make self-defeating humor the emotional payoff,** even though it spreads (Schneider et al. 2018).
- **Let parody dark patterns become real friction,** or front-load the logo (Tellis et al. 2019).
- **Treat "arousal = virality" as settled** (Prowten et al. 2024).

## What I could not verify
